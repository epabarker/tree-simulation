# Beech: what the photographs show (geometry note, 2026-11-01)

Written from Wikimedia Commons photographs looked at in a browser; the images are linked, not
copied, and the descriptions are the writer's own reading of them. They are a supplement to the
sourced identity card (`species-identity-beech.md`), for anyone who cannot open the links. The
proportions are estimates by eye from single photographs, good to about ±20%.

## 1. Open-grown, mature, leaf-off: the dome
[Entzia - Haya 01.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Entzia_-_Haya_01.jpg)
(Basque hills, a lone beech on pasture, winter)

- **Proportion:** wider than high, W/H ≈ 1.3–1.5. The outline is a broad dome, highest near the
  middle, rounded, with no point and no leader visible anywhere.
- **Trunk:** short and stout, about 1/8–1/6 of the height, then it **divides within a short zone**
  into roughly 5–7 major limbs. Above that zone there is no trunk: the limbs are the tree.
- **Limbs:** of similar thickness to each other (none dominant). The central ones leave at
  20–40° from vertical, the outer ones at 50–70°; the lowest run near horizontal from the base
  and droop at their ends. Every limb **forks repeatedly along its length**, each fork giving two
  axes of nearly equal size (a sympodial look), so the limb's path is a chain of straight segments
  with a small change of direction at each fork, bending outward overall, the outer twigs
  **turning up slightly at the tips**.
- **Twigs:** very fine and very dense; the periphery reads as a grey haze rather than as
  individual twigs. That haze is the dome's outline.
- **Thickness hierarchy:** gentle: trunk ≫ limbs, but limbs ≈ each other and each fork's two
  daughters ≈ each other.

## 2. A released stand tree, leaf-on, spring
[Fagus sylvatica TK 2023-05-06 1.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fagus_sylvatica_TK_2023-05-06_1.jpg)
(a beech left standing after the wood around it was felled)

- **Proportion:** about as wide as high, W/H ≈ 0.9–1.0; the crown occupies the top ≈ 60% of the
  height, the lower 40% a clean straight bole (the stand's legacy).
- **Trunk:** at about 1/3 of the height it divides into 2–3 steep limbs that then fan out and
  arch; a few thin, near-level old limbs remain lower on the bole.
- **Crown:** broad and rounded, slightly flat-topped, built of **level, overlapping sprays**; the
  foliage reads as horizontal layers stacked up the crown, not as tiers on a cone (the layers do
  not get shorter towards a point; the top is as wide as the middle).

## 3. Stand-grown, leaf-off
[Fagus sylvatica sl10.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fagus_sylvatica_sl10.jpg) and
its two neighbours in the same search (Lower Austria, a dry wood)

- **Trunk:** straight and clean for the lower half or more, then it **divides into several steep
  limbs** (20–40° from vertical) that go on dividing; the crown is a rounded broom of steep
  limbs, **rounded at the top**, never pointed.
- **Branches:** crooked and fine; many near-equal axes rather than one leader with laterals. The
  crown is narrow (W/H ≈ 0.3–0.4) but its top is round.
- In a closed stand ([Boitzenburger Wald, winter](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Boitzenburger-Wald-16-02-2009-13.jpg))
  the canopy trees are poles with high crowns, and the **understorey beeches keep fine, dense,
  low branching** all the way down: the stand floor is not bare.

## 4. Ancient open-grown
[Fagus sylvatica - Nisramont - JPG1.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Fagus_sylvatica_-_Nisramont_-_JPG1.jpg)

- A massive short trunk, several very thick limbs leaving near the base at 40–80°, the lowest
  near horizontal for many metres; the fine twigs along the limbs' upper sides point up.

## What this means for the picture, at 20 m

The cue that separates beech from the current render is not the angle of any one limb but
**the loss of the trunk into several near-equal, repeatedly forking limbs**, each bending outward
along its length and ending in a dense haze of fine twigs. That produces the dome (open-grown),
the rounded broom (stand), and the level layers of foliage (the sprays sit along the limbs'
upper sides). A single persistent leader with laterals of decreasing length — the cone — is the
one thing none of these photographs shows, at any age or in any setting.
