# How real trees self-prune to a bare bole (issue #75, wayfinder research, 2026-11-08)

Question ([#75](https://github.com/epabarker/tree-simulation/issues/75)): what makes the lower branches die and fall, leaving a clear bole; what threshold, what autonomy, how long from death to fall; how side light and stand density change it; which species do it readily; what numbers can anchor the model.

Not repeated here: the C5 criterion review in [2026-10-27-literature-review.md](2026-10-27-literature-review.md) (Sprugel 2002, Henriksson 2001, Kothari 2025, Schoonmaker 2014 abstracts) and the crown-ratio numbers in [2026-10-28-species-traits.md](2026-10-28-species-traits.md) (Pretzsch 2019). This note adds what they lack: the death-to-fall lag, the side-light evidence, density effects and the species contrasts. Context: [ADR 0043](../decisions/0043-own-crown-pruning.md) (the user sees the lower branches always survive; the Tvig fix was rejected).

## 1. What kills a lower branch

**1.1 Carbon balance against a light threshold, but the threshold is not fixed.**
- Kothari et al. 2025 (*Functional Ecology* 39(9):2234-2250, doi 10.1111/1365-2435.70116) [OA]: 12 North American species in the IDENT-Montreal plantation (planted 2009, measured July-August 2018, so trees were about 9 years old and 2-8 m tall; 546 trees). Lbase = ln(I_base / I_open) is the light just below the **live crown base**, taken as the self-pruning threshold. Sensors were three horizontal upward-facing quantum sensors on a plate held parallel to the ground, 4 compass directions x 3 rotations, clear sky, 9:00-16:00, median solar zenith 36.6 degrees. So the reading is mostly vertical, with some oblique sun.
  - Species identity alone explains 51.3% of Lbase variance. Lbase is lower in the six evergreens than in the six deciduous species (including the deciduous larch, which behaved like the broadleaves). Species-level correlations: acquisitiveness R2 = 0.655 (positive), shade tolerance R2 = 0.411 (negative), log leaf lifespan R2 = 0.846 (negative; 0.824 within evergreens only); tolerance plus acquisitiveness together R2 = 0.897. Shade tolerance and acquisitiveness are uncorrelated here (R2 = 0.038).
  - Within species, Lbase rises with light at the crown top (Ltop; p = 0.002; no significant species interaction, p = 0.099), which the authors read as correlative inhibition. **Correction to the first pass:** the height effect is not a clean "rises with height". Lbase vs tree height is p = 0.027, but the species slopes differ (interaction p < 0.001) and track acquisitiveness (R2 = 0.786): acquisitive species rise with height, conservative evergreens fall. The authors also flag a confound: a neighbour dying lifts light at both top and base without any change in pruning strategy. They did not find that shade tolerance modifies the Ltop effect.
  - **Definition caveat the first pass missed:** the crown base is where continuous live foliage begins, not where dead branches detach. Lbase measures where the live crown ends; it says nothing about death-to-fall timing, and the paper says epicormic sprouting was seldom seen.
  - Per-species Lbase values are plotted in Figure 2 only; the article text has no table and the supplement was not opened. Species: Abies balsamea, Acer rubrum, A. saccharum, Betula alleghaniensis, B. papyrifera, Larix laricina, Picea glauca, P. rubens, Pinus resinosa, P. strobus, Quercus rubra, Thuja occidentalis (the first pass omitted Abies balsamea). No European beech, ash, hornbeam or holly: confirmed, nothing for the garden species.
  - The authors say the premise (branches are kept or pruned according to carbon balance) is untested against water relations and other co-limits, and note some shade-intolerant pines transmit more than 25% light at high stocking.
- Schoonmaker et al. 2014 (*PLoS ONE* 9:e104187, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0104187) [PMC]: field trial on about 17-year-old lodgepole pine and white spruce, 3.3-4.5 m tall, 10 sites, two seasons of shading. Lower-crown light as a share of full light: unshaded 57.9%, whole tree shaded 10.8%, lower quarter-to-third shaded 7.1% (light) and 2.9% (heavy). **Correction:** the response measured was bud expansion and shoot growth, not branch death. In pine lower branches, bud expansion was about 40% under asymmetric shading, about 80% under uniform shading and 100% unshaded; shoot growth fell more than 50% under asymmetric shading. Spruce was affected less. Lower-branch needle carbohydrate in unshaded pine was 17% (spruce nearly 30%); asymmetric shading lowered lower-branch needle carbohydrate relative to the upper crown, read as reserves moving away or less being made (the authors favour less being made). Their conclusion is that the results support "a carbon based mechanism for branch mortality mediated by an asymmetry in light exposure", which is looser than the first pass's "hurt lower branches more". Juvenile conifers only.

**1.2 Branch autonomy is partial; branches compete with each other.**
- Henriksson 2001 (*Oecologia* 126:482-486, doi 10.1007/s004420000547) [abs]: mountain birch (*Betula pubescens* ssp. *czerepanovii*). After two summers 90% of individually shaded branches were dead; none died when the whole tree was shaded. Author's reading: fate depends on asymmetric competition among branches, not shade alone.
- Sprugel 2002 (*Tree Physiol.* 22:1119-1124, doi 10.1093/treephys/22.15-16.1119) [abs, PubMed abstract]: the abstract says correlative inhibition matters more than autonomy: branches on suppressed trees grow and make new foliage at irradiances where branches on dominant trees die, so "a positive carbon budget by itself does not ensure branch survival"; position relative to other branches matters. It generalises to other stresses. The first pass had this second-hand; the abstract confirms it. Schoonmaker's introduction [PMC] also cites Sprugel for the amabilis fir case (a fully shaded suppressed tree kept branches that a dominant would lose).
- Chen & Sumida 2017 (*Forests* 8:26) [snip]: in a Sakhalin spruce plantation, death probability of a whole primary branch fell the further it was above the crown base (p = 0.04) but was not significantly related to relative PPFD above it (p = 0.18). Chen et al. 2018 (*Trees*) [snip]: for primary branches, crown position matters as much as local light; for secondary branches light dominates. So light alone does not decide who dies.
- Majekobaje et al. 2023 (*Forests*) [snip]: in loblolly pine, a PCA of photosynthesis and water traits flagged branches that would die about four months before death was visible. Death is a slow decline, not a switch.

**1.3 Shade tolerance sets how deep the crown stays.**
- Kothari (above); Touzot et al. 2025 (*Oikos*) [snip]: shade-tolerant species have wider, deeper crowns and relative crown depth is less sensitive to local competition. This is the opposite of "tolerant species form a clear bole" (see section 5). Kothari's Lbase result agrees: tolerant, conservative species prune under deeper shade.

## 2. Death to fall: how long the dead branch stays

The model removes a shed branch at once. Real branches go through death, a standing dead stub, then fall, and the lag differs by species.

- **Kint, Hein, Campioli & Muys 2010** (*Forest Ecol. Manage.* 260:2023-2034, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2010.09.008) [abs: ScienceDirect abstract, highlights and introduction snippet]: 399 oak and 376 beech trees in 30 plots per species in two Flemish forests, **young planted, even-aged stands, models for the lowest 6 m of stem only**. **Correction:** the first pass described these as stands generally; they are young plantations and the 6 m limit is the timber-quality zone, so the result is about early self-pruning, not mature-bole clearing. Finding: "similar self-pruning rates in oak and beech, but with different driving factors: early branch dying and slow shedding for oak and the other way around for beech". Four processes: stand and tree development, competitive status, stand density, site humidity; branch mortality also depends on branch size and position. No years are given in what was readable, and the paywalled results were not seen, so the exact lags are unknown. The first pass's wording ("oak keeps the dead ones, beech drops them fast") restates the abstract and stands, flagged abstract-only.
- **Dead-branch portion inside the stem (indirect lag evidence):** Hein 2008 (*Forest Ecol. Manage.* 256:2046-2057, beech, 41 trees, 717 branches) [abs] and Hein & Spiecker 2007 (*Can. J. For. Res.*, doi 10.1139/X06-308, ash and sycamore, 635 and 334 branches) [abs] model the time to full occlusion of a dead branch stub and the "loose knot" (dead) portion from branch diameter and stem radial increment. Larger branches take longer to occlude and leave more dead portion (beech). Only a few species differences were found between ash and sycamore. The dead portion of a knot records how long a dead stub stood before being enclosed, so these papers hold the closest thing to years from death to loss, but the abstracts give no figures [u for numbers].
- **Anatomical cladoptosis** (shedding at an abscission or brittle zone) exists for twigs and some branches: Schaffner & Tyler 1901 (*Ohio Naturalist* 1(3), via search summary) [snip] describe single-joint shedding in *Populus alba* and *Salix nigra* (clean cuts) and shed twigs 1-4 years old in *Quercus alba*. Old and weakly sourced; no modern review found [u].
- **Fall of dead stems** (the only quantified fall data found; branches are thinner, so their lag is shorter): almost all dead birch, pine and spruce stems fell within 40 years, birch fastest (Makinen et al. 2006, *Ecol. Appl.* [snip]); standing-dead time to fall falls with temperature and is longer for conifers (Gartner et al. 2023, *Forests* [snip]; Oberle et al. 2018, *PLoS ONE* [snip]); beech wood has the shortest residence time of Swiss species, 27-54 years (Hararuk et al. 2020, *Forest Ecosyst.* [snip]). **No primary source with branch-level years from death to fall was found, in either pass [u]** (a further search for stub persistence in beech, oak and ash returned only occlusion and arboricultural pruning material). The usable result is the ranking: broadleaf faster than conifer, warm faster than cold, beech fast, oak slow (Kint).
- Epicormic shoots complicate "bare": in pedunculate oak, new epicormic shoots on the lower stem (0-3 m) rise with stand density and after pruning (Attocchi 2013, *Ann. For. Sci.* [snip]).

## 3. Stand density and thinning

- Makela 1997 (*Forest Sci.* 43:7-24) [snip]: in a Scots pine carbon-balance model the pruning height follows a self-pruning coefficient made a function of crown coverage; lower stocking gives larger crown ratios.
- Beech: Reventlow et al. 2018 (*Forestry*) [snip]: the lowest live branch sits lower with heavy thinning. The related Danish study (Zhu 2005 thesis summary [snip]): "natural pruning was hampered by increasing thinning grade".
- Oak: young stands are kept "quite dense for good natural pruning", and a beech, hornbeam and lime understorey "promotes the natural shedding of lower branches of the oak" (Nicolescu et al. 2025, *J. For. Res.* [snip]).
- Birch plantations: natural pruning rate rises with planting density, most abscission in the leaf-fall season (Wang et al. 2026, *Trees For. People*, *Betula alnoides* [snip]).

## 4. Side light: edges and gaps

Evidence is observational; a direct experiment on lateral light and branch survival was not found [u]. The first pass had no authors or journals for these; they are now captured.
- **Seidel, Ruzicka & Puettmann 2016**, *Forest Ecol. Manage.* 363:31-38, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2015.12.024 [repo: Oregon State Andrews Forest PDF, full text]. Douglas-fir plantation in the western Cascades, thinned to 275 trees/ha with 0.2 ha artificial gaps; terrestrial laser scanning of 20 border trees and 20 interior trees 18 years after gap creation. Table 1 (border vs interior): crown base height 9.83 vs 15.59 m (p < 0.001); live crown ratio 73.7% vs 59.0%; crown length 27.5 vs 22.5 m; crown asymmetry directed to the gap, strongest in the lowest third of the crown. The authors conclude border trees have shorter branch-free boles and larger knots. This is the one quantified side-light case: a conifer with a 6 m crown-base difference. They note earlier work found similar asymmetry in maple, birch, ash, oak and European beech (Muth & Bazzaz 2003; Seidel et al. 2011b), not read.
- **Sherich, Pocewicz & Morgan 2007**, *Can. J. For. Res.*, doi 10.1139/X07-105 [abs]: 23 forest edges older than 50 years in northern Idaho, ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir. Edge trees had more leaf area, deeper crowns and more sapwood than interior trees, and greater canopy depth on the edge-facing side. This replaces the first pass's "uief_0189" citation.
- **Salek, Zahradnik, Marusak, Jerabkova & Merganic 2013**, *Forest Ecol. Manage.*, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2013.05.012 [abs]: managed riparian forests near Kromeriz, Czech Republic, 50 transects. The edge zone is 4-18 m wide, mean 8 m, for basal area, stand volume, slenderness and similar stand metrics. Height of the first green branch and crown length ratio were measured, but the abstract states no crown result, so this is evidence for edge width in European broadleaf forest, not for branch retention. **Correction:** the first pass listed it as crown-edge evidence.
- The sand pine edge study cited in the first pass could not be re-found (a targeted search returned only species descriptions); drop it unless located. The gap-border claim in the first pass is now Seidel 2016.
- Mechanism: Henriksson, Sprugel and Schoonmaker say a branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours, so a limb lit from the side is not in deficit even if the sky above is blocked. Kothari's Lbase is a mostly vertical reading and cannot separate this. Seidel's numbers support the direction (lower crown base on the lit side), not the mechanism.

## 5. Species: who clears and who keeps

| Species | What the evidence says | Source and strength |
|---|---|---|
| Beech | Branches die late, then fall fast. Stand clear-bole fraction (1 - crown ratio) about 0.34-0.43 for h 20-30 m. Open-grown, clear only for the lowest 1/8-1/6 of height. | Kint 2010 [abs, young stands to 6 m]; Pretzsch 2019 via the 10-28 note; ADR 0043 |
| Oak | Light-demanding; branches die early but dead ones persist; relies on a shade-tolerant understorey to clear; epicormics. Crown ratio about 0.42, clear bole about 0.58. | Kint 2010 [abs]; Nicolescu 2025 [snip]; Pretzsch via 10-28 |
| Ash | Different allocation pattern from the other five species on a European tolerance gradient. No branch-death data; Hein & Spiecker 2007 [abs] has ash branch-occlusion models but no readable figures. | Bartkowicz et al. 2023 [snip]; [u] |
| Hornbeam | Very shade-tolerant understorey tree. No branch data. | Bartkowicz 2023 [snip]; [u] |
| Holly | **No source found [u].** Evergreen, long leaf life; the Lbase-leaf-lifespan relation (R2 = 0.85) predicts a very low threshold and a deep crown, which fits keeping lower branches. | Inference from Kothari [OA], not holly data. A second web search for holly or hornbeam branch retention found only horticultural pages |
| Firs, spruces | Evergreen, low Lbase, deep crowns; shade-tolerant white spruce was hurt less than lodgepole pine by lower-crown shading. Dead conifer wood stands longer than broadleaf (stems only). | Kothari [OA]; Schoonmaker [PMC, juvenile pine and spruce]; Gartner, Oberle [snip] |
| Open-grown (any) | Retain branches. | Sections 3-4 |

Caution on "shade-tolerant means clear bole": the data say the live crown of tolerant species stays deeper (Kothari, Touzot). Beech clears its bole in dense stands because it is dense and because its dead limbs drop quickly; oak clears with help from companions.

## Implications for the model

**Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence.**
1. **A branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours and the tree can do without it (rank, not absolute light).** Strongest: Henriksson (90% vs 0%, abstract), Sprugel (abstract), Kothari's Ltop effect (full text, p = 0.002, but confounded by neighbour death and young trees), Schoonmaker (full text, but juvenile conifers and bud/shoot response, not death). C5's T_rel (ADR 0007) is relative, but to the open sky. This evidence says the threshold should rise with the tree's own top light, which is what ADR 0043's vigour gate reached for without a source. It is a cited alternative to Tvig, not a licence to bring Tvig back; it is a C5 change and needs the user.
2. **Species threshold set by leaf life and shade tolerance (Lbase).** Strong, quantified (full text), but only as per-species means in a figure, in a different unit from the model's Q, from a 9-year-old plantation, and silent on European species. The measure is the live-crown base, so it fits the model's visible bole height but not the dead-stub lag. Beech's T_rel of 0.008 is a shade-leaf figure (Hagemeier & Leuschner, per ADR 0043); Lbase is a branch-death figure. They are different measures and may differ a lot. Test before blaming C5.
3. **Death-to-fall lag, species-specific.** Moderate to weak: Kint gives direction only, from the abstract, for young plantations below 6 m. Hein's occlusion models may hold stub-duration numbers but sit behind paywalls. The model has no dead-stub state, so shedding is instant. A lag would leave oak's dead stubs visible longer and beech's bole clean.
4. **Stand density, via crown coverage.** Moderate (Makela, Reventlow, Nicolescu). Density already acts through light in the model, so this is a check, not a rule: does the lower crown see less light as the stand closes?
5. **Branch position or distance to crown base.** Weak to moderate (Chen & Sumida); probably a proxy for light history.
6. **Anatomical cladoptosis.** Weak (old sources, poplar and willow). Not a candidate for the garden species.

**Role of side light.** A frontal slab with a vertical light sweep may credit a lower limb with side light from the slab edge or from gaps between neighbours. Real edge and gap trees keep lower limbs, and the rank rule says why: a sideways-lit branch is not darker than its neighbours. If lower limbs survive in the model's stands, side light is a likelier cause than the threshold. The only quantified case is Seidel 2016 (full text): Douglas-fir gap-border trees had a crown base 5.8 m lower than interior trees (9.8 vs 15.6 m), with the lower crown skewed to the gap. It is a conifer, 18 years after gap creation, 20 trees per group; the rest is abstract-level.

**Diagnostics to run before changing any rule** (all are measurements):
1. For each stand tree at run end, plot the light per metamer (C5's measure) of the lowest living limb against the species' T and T_rel. ADR 0043 found the lone beech's limbs 14-80x above threshold; repeat for stand trees.
2. Split that light into vertical and lateral parts: how much of a surviving lower limb's light enters from the side (slab edge, neighbours' gaps)? If most, side light is the cause.
3. Compare the model's clear-bole fraction (lowest living limb height over tree height) with 1 - CR: beech 0.34-0.43, oak about 0.58, over at least 20 seeds with the pass rate stated (AGENTS.md).
4. Measure crown-base height over tree height in the Wytham Woods TLS data named in the [reference-data note](2026-11-03-reference-data-and-evaluation.md) (sycamore, oak, ash, hazel) to get real clear-bole fractions for three garden species from full data.
5. A switch test: shed instantly versus a fall lag of n seasons for dead limbs, to see whether visible bole height changes (it should for oak, little for beech).
6. Before any rank-based shedding code, analyse recorded runs: which limbs below the tree's own median limb light would have been shed?

**Open gaps** (candidates for the map): branch-level death-to-fall years for beech, oak, ash, hornbeam (still none after the second pass); holly and hornbeam branch data (none); a direct lateral-light experiment; per-species Lbase values (Kothari Figure 2 is a plot; the supplement and data repository were not opened, and the authors may supply the numbers); an Lbase-like measure for European broadleaves.

**What the second pass changed in these conclusions:** none of the ranking moved. Three claims were narrowed: Kint is young plantations to 6 m, Schoonmaker measured bud and shoot growth on juvenile conifers, and Kothari's height effect depends on species. The Czech edge study is not crown evidence.
