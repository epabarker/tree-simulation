# How real trees self-prune to a bare bole (issue #75, wayfinder research, 2026-11-08)

Question ([#75](https://github.com/epabarker/tree-simulation/issues/75)): what makes the lower branches die and fall, leaving a clear bole; what threshold, what autonomy, how long from death to fall; how side light and stand density change it; which species do it readily; what numbers can anchor the model.

Not repeated here: the C5 criterion review in [2026-10-27-literature-review.md](2026-10-27-literature-review.md) (Sprugel 2002, Henriksson 2001, Kothari 2025, Schoonmaker 2014 abstracts) and the crown-ratio numbers in [2026-10-28-species-traits.md](2026-10-28-species-traits.md) (Pretzsch 2019). This note adds what they lack: the death-to-fall lag, the side-light evidence, density effects and the species contrasts. Context: [ADR 0043](../decisions/0043-own-crown-pruning.md) (the user sees the lower branches always survive; the Tvig fix was rejected).

## 1. What kills a lower branch

**1.1 Carbon balance against a light threshold, but the threshold is not fixed.**
- Kothari et al. 2025 (*Functional Ecology*, doi 10.1111/1365-2435.70116) [r]: 12 temperate species in the IDENT-Montreal plantation. They measured the light fraction just below the crown base, Lbase = ln(I_base / I_open), taken as the self-pruning threshold. Sensors read upward (3 sensors, 4 compass directions, 3 rotations), at solar zenith about 25-55 degrees, so the reading is mostly vertical with some side light.
  - Species identity alone explains 51% of Lbase variance. Lbase is lower in the six evergreens than in the six deciduous species. It correlates with shade tolerance (R2 = 0.41), leaf lifespan (R2 = 0.85, negative) and acquisitiveness (R2 = 0.66); tolerance plus acquisitiveness together give R2 = 0.90.
  - Within species, Lbase rises with light at the crown top (Ltop; p = 0.002) and with tree height (p = 0.027). Read: a tree with a bright top prunes its lower crown at a brighter threshold. That is correlative inhibition, not pure autonomy.
  - Per-species Lbase values appear only in Figure 2; no table was recovered. Species include Acer rubrum, A. saccharum, Betula alleghaniensis, B. papyrifera, Larix laricina, Picea glauca, P. rubens, Pinus resinosa, P. strobus, Quercus rubra, Thuja occidentalis. No European beech, ash or hornbeam.
- Schoonmaker et al. 2014 (*PLoS ONE*) [a]: shading only the lower quarter to third of the crown hurt lower branches more than shading the whole tree, and more in shade-intolerant lodgepole pine than in white spruce. Lower branches held less stored carbohydrate, which suggests reserves move away from them. Supports a carbon mechanism driven by asymmetry, not by absolute light.

**1.2 Branch autonomy is partial; branches compete with each other.**
- Henriksson 2001 (*Oecologia* 126:482-486) [a]: mountain birch (*Betula pubescens* ssp. *czerepanovii*). After two summers 90% of individually shaded branches were dead; none died when the whole tree was shaded. Author's reading: fate depends on asymmetric competition among branches, not shade alone.
- Sprugel 2002 (*Tree Physiol.* 22:1119-1124) [a, via later papers]: autonomy is "not an absolute rule"; branches on suppressed trees survive where the same branches on dominants die (already in the 10-27 review).
- Chen & Sumida 2017 (*Forests* 8:26) [a]: in a Sakhalin spruce plantation, death probability of a whole primary branch fell the further it was above the crown base (p = 0.04) but was not significantly related to relative PPFD above it (p = 0.18). Chen et al. 2018 (*Trees*) [a]: for primary branches, crown position matters as much as local light; for secondary branches light dominates. So light alone does not decide who dies.
- Majekobaje et al. 2023 (*Forests*) [a]: in loblolly pine, a PCA of photosynthesis and water traits flagged branches that would die about four months before death was visible. Death is a slow decline, not a switch.

**1.3 Shade tolerance sets how deep the crown stays.**
- Kothari (above); Touzot et al. 2025 (*Oikos*) [a]: shade-tolerant species have wider, deeper crowns and relative crown depth is less sensitive to local competition. This is the opposite of "tolerant species form a clear bole" (see section 5).

## 2. Death to fall: how long the dead branch stays

The model removes a shed branch at once. Real branches go through death, a standing dead stub, then fall, and the lag differs by species.

- **Kint et al. 2010** (*Forest Ecol. Manage.*) [a], 399 oak and 376 beech trees in Flemish stands, models to 6 m height: "similar self-pruning rates in oak and beech, but with different driving factors: early branch dying and slow shedding for oak and the other way around for beech." Oak kills its lower branches early but keeps the dead ones; beech keeps branches alive longer and drops them fast once dead. Four drivers named: stand and tree development, competitive status, stand density, site humidity. Branch death also depends on position and size. No years in the abstract.
- **Anatomical cladoptosis** (shedding at an abscission or brittle zone) exists for twigs and some branches: Schaffner & Tyler 1901 (*Ohio Naturalist* 1(3), via search summary) [a] describe single-joint shedding in *Populus alba* and *Salix nigra* (clean cuts) and shed twigs 1-4 years old in *Quercus alba*. Old and weakly sourced; no modern review found [u].
- **Fall of dead stems** (the only quantified fall data found; branches are thinner, so their lag is shorter): almost all dead birch, pine and spruce stems fell within 40 years, birch fastest (Makinen et al. 2006, *Ecol. Appl.* [a]); standing-dead time to fall falls with temperature and is longer for conifers (Gartner et al. 2023, *Forests* [a]; Oberle et al. 2018, *PLoS ONE* [a]); beech wood has the shortest residence time of Swiss species, 27-54 years (Hararuk et al. 2020, *Forest Ecosyst.* [a]). **No primary source with branch-level years from death to fall was found [u].** The usable result is the ranking: broadleaf faster than conifer, warm faster than cold, beech fast, oak slow (Kint).
- Epicormic shoots complicate "bare": in pedunculate oak, new epicormic shoots on the lower stem (0-3 m) rise with stand density and after pruning (Attocchi 2013, *Ann. For. Sci.* [a]).

## 3. Stand density and thinning

- Makela 1997 (*Forest Sci.* 43:7-24) [a]: in a Scots pine carbon-balance model the pruning height follows a self-pruning coefficient made a function of crown coverage; lower stocking gives larger crown ratios.
- Beech: Reventlow et al. 2018 (*Forestry*) [a]: the lowest live branch sits lower with heavy thinning. The related Danish study (Zhu 2005 thesis summary [a]): "natural pruning was hampered by increasing thinning grade".
- Oak: young stands are kept "quite dense for good natural pruning", and a beech, hornbeam and lime understorey "promotes the natural shedding of lower branches of the oak" (Nicolescu et al. 2025, *J. For. Res.* [a]).
- Birch plantations: natural pruning rate rises with planting density, most abscission in the leaf-fall season (Wang et al. 2026, *Trees For. People*, *Betula alnoides* [a]).

## 4. Side light: edges and gaps

Evidence is thin and observational; a direct experiment on lateral light and branch survival was not found [u].
- Edge conifers: sand pine edge trees "retained branches lower on their boles" with crowns asymmetric toward the edge, a modest effect (*Pinus clausa* edge study, 2021 [a]). In Idaho ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir, edge trees had deeper crowns, deeper on the edge-facing side (University of Idaho edge study, uief_0189 [a]).
- Gap-border trees in thinned stands: crown asymmetry, strongest in the lower third, points toward the gap; border trees have shorter branch-free boles and larger knots (search summary [a]).
- Edge influence in a Czech riparian forest reached 4-18 m, mean 8 m [a].
- Mechanism: Henriksson and Schoonmaker say a branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours, so a limb lit from the side is not in deficit even if the sky above is blocked. Kothari's Lbase is a mostly vertical reading and cannot separate this.

## 5. Species: who clears and who keeps

| Species | What the evidence says | Source and strength |
|---|---|---|
| Beech | Branches die late, then fall fast. Stand clear-bole fraction (1 - crown ratio) about 0.34-0.43 for h 20-30 m. Open-grown, clear only for the lowest 1/8-1/6 of height. | Kint 2010 [a]; Pretzsch 2019 via the 10-28 note [r]; ADR 0043 |
| Oak | Light-demanding; branches die early but dead ones persist; relies on a shade-tolerant understorey to clear; epicormics. Crown ratio about 0.42, clear bole about 0.58. | Kint 2010 [a]; Nicolescu 2025 [a]; Pretzsch via 10-28 [r] |
| Ash | Different allocation pattern from the other five species on a European tolerance gradient. No branch-death data. | Bartkowicz et al. 2023 [a]; [u] |
| Hornbeam | Very shade-tolerant understorey tree. No branch data. | Bartkowicz 2023 [a]; [u] |
| Holly | **No source found [u].** Evergreen, long leaf life; the Lbase-leaf-lifespan relation (R2 = 0.85) predicts a very low threshold and a deep crown, which fits keeping lower branches. | Inference from Kothari [r], not holly data |
| Firs, spruces | Evergreen, low Lbase, deep crowns; shade-tolerant white spruce was hurt less than lodgepole pine by lower-crown shading. Dead conifer wood stands longer than broadleaf (stems only). | Kothari [r]; Schoonmaker [a]; Gartner, Oberle [a] |
| Open-grown (any) | Retain branches. | Sections 3-4 |

Caution on "shade-tolerant means clear bole": the data say the live crown of tolerant species stays deeper (Kothari, Touzot). Beech clears its bole in dense stands because it is dense and because its dead limbs drop quickly; oak clears with help from companions.

## 6. Anchors the model can use

| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|

| Individually shaded branch, mountain birch | 90% dead in two summers; 0% if whole tree shaded | Henriksson 2001 [a] |
| Beech vs oak lag | oak: early death, slow shedding; beech: late death, fast shedding | Kint 2010 [a] |
| Stand crown ratio | beech 0.57 +- 0.08, oak 0.42 +- 0.08 | 10-28 note (Pretzsch 2019) |
| Open-grown beech clear bole | about 1/8 to 1/6 of height | ADR 0043 photograph note |
| Sapling mortality, beech/ash/maple | near zero at about 15% of canopy light | Petritan et al. 2007 [a] |
| Years from branch death to fall | not found; dead birch stems all fallen within 40 y | [u]; Makinen 2006 [a] |

## Implications for the model

**Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence.**
1. **A branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours and the tree can do without it (rank, not absolute light).** Strongest: Henriksson (90% vs 0%), Sprugel, Kothari's Ltop effect, Schoonmaker. C5's T_rel (ADR 0007) is relative, but to the open sky. This evidence says the threshold should rise with the tree's own top light, which is what ADR 0043's vigour gate reached for without a source. It is a cited alternative to Tvig, not a licence to bring Tvig back; it is a C5 change and needs the user.
2. **Species threshold set by leaf life and shade tolerance (Lbase).** Strong, quantified, but only as per-species means in a different unit from the model's Q, and silent on European species. Beech's T_rel of 0.008 is a shade-leaf figure (Hagemeier & Leuschner, per ADR 0043); Lbase is a branch-death figure. They are different measures and may differ a lot. Test before blaming C5.
3. **Death-to-fall lag, species-specific.** Moderate: Kint gives direction only. The model has no dead-stub state, so shedding is instant. A lag would leave oak's dead stubs visible longer and beech's bole clean.
4. **Stand density, via crown coverage.** Moderate (Makela, Reventlow, Nicolescu). Density already acts through light in the model, so this is a check, not a rule: does the lower crown see less light as the stand closes?
5. **Branch position or distance to crown base.** Weak to moderate (Chen & Sumida); probably a proxy for light history.
6. **Anatomical cladoptosis.** Weak (old sources, poplar and willow). Not a candidate for the garden species.

**Role of side light.** A frontal slab with a vertical light sweep may credit a lower limb with side light from the slab edge or from gaps between neighbours. Real edge and gap trees keep lower limbs, and the rank rule says why: a sideways-lit branch is not darker than its neighbours. If lower limbs survive in the model's stands, side light is a likelier cause than the threshold. The edge and gap evidence is observational and unquantified.

**Diagnostics to run before changing any rule** (all are measurements):
1. For each stand tree at run end, plot the light per metamer (C5's measure) of the lowest living limb against the species' T and T_rel. ADR 0043 found the lone beech's limbs 14-80x above threshold; repeat for stand trees.
2. Split that light into vertical and lateral parts: how much of a surviving lower limb's light enters from the side (slab edge, neighbours' gaps)? If most, side light is the cause.
3. Compare the model's clear-bole fraction (lowest living limb height over tree height) with 1 - CR: beech 0.34-0.43, oak about 0.58, over at least 20 seeds with the pass rate stated (AGENTS.md).
4. Measure crown-base height over tree height in the Wytham Woods TLS data named in the [reference-data note](2026-11-03-reference-data-and-evaluation.md) (sycamore, oak, ash, hazel) to get real clear-bole fractions for three garden species from full data.
5. A switch test: shed instantly versus a fall lag of n seasons for dead limbs, to see whether visible bole height changes (it should for oak, little for beech).
6. Before any rank-based shedding code, analyse recorded runs: which limbs below the tree's own median limb light would have been shed?

**Open gaps** (candidates for the map): branch-level death-to-fall years for beech, oak, ash, hornbeam; holly branch data; a direct lateral-light experiment; per-species Lbase for European species (Kothari Figure 2 values not extracted).
