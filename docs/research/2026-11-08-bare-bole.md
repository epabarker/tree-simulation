# How real trees self-prune to a bare bole (issue #75, wayfinder research, 2026-11-08)

Question ([#75](https://github.com/epabarker/tree-simulation/issues/75)): what makes the lower branches die and fall, leaving a clear bole; what threshold, what autonomy, how long from death to fall; how side light and stand density change it; which species do it readily; what numbers can anchor the model.

Not repeated here: the C5 criterion review in [2026-10-27-literature-review.md](2026-10-27-literature-review.md) (Sprugel 2002, Henriksson 2001, Kothari 2025, Schoonmaker 2014 abstracts) and the crown-ratio numbers in [2026-10-28-species-traits.md](2026-10-28-species-traits.md) (Pretzsch 2019). This note adds what they lack: the death-to-fall lag, the side-light evidence, density effects and the species contrasts. Context: [ADR 0043](../decisions/0043-own-crown-pruning.md) (the user sees the lower branches always survive; the Tvig fix was rejected).

## 1. What kills a lower branch

**1.1 Carbon balance against a light threshold, but the threshold is not fixed.**
- Kothari et al. 2025 (*Functional Ecology* 39(9):2234-2250, doi 10.1111/1365-2435.70116) [OA]: 12 North American species in the IDENT-Montreal plantation (planted 2009, measured July-August 2018, so trees were about 9 years old and 2-8 m tall; 546 trees). Lbase = ln(I_base / I_open) is the light just below the **live crown base**, taken as the self-pruning threshold. Sensors were three horizontal upward-facing quantum sensors on a plate held parallel to the ground, 4 compass directions x 3 rotations, clear sky, 9:00-16:00, median solar zenith 36.6 degrees. So the reading is mostly vertical, with some oblique sun.
  - Species identity alone explains 51.3% of Lbase variance. Lbase is lower in the six evergreens than in the six deciduous species (including the deciduous larch, which behaved like the broadleaves). Species-level correlations: acquisitiveness R2 = 0.655 (positive), shade tolerance R2 = 0.411 (negative), log leaf lifespan R2 = 0.846 (negative; 0.824 within evergreens only); tolerance plus acquisitiveness together R2 = 0.897. Shade tolerance and acquisitiveness are uncorrelated here (R2 = 0.038).
  - Within species, Lbase rises with light at the crown top (Ltop; p = 0.002; no significant species interaction, p = 0.099), which the authors read as correlative inhibition. **Correction to the first pass:** the height effect is not a clean "rises with height". Lbase vs tree height is p = 0.027, but the species slopes differ (interaction p < 0.001) and track acquisitiveness (R2 = 0.786): acquisitive species rise with height, conservative evergreens fall. The authors also flag a confound: a neighbour dying lifts light at both top and base without any change in pruning strategy. They did not find that shade tolerance modifies the Ltop effect.
  - **Definition caveat the first pass missed:** the crown base is where continuous live foliage begins, not where dead branches detach. Lbase measures where the live crown ends; it says nothing about death-to-fall timing, and the paper says epicormic sprouting was seldom seen.
  - Per-species Lbase values are plotted in Figure 2 only; the article text has no table and the supplement was not opened. Species: Abies balsamea, Acer rubrum, A. saccharum, Betula alleghaniensis, B. papyrifera, Larix laricina, Picea glauca, P. rubens, Pinus resinosa, P. strobus, Quercus rubra, Thuja occidentalis (the first pass omitted Abies balsamea). No European beech, ash, hornbeam or holly: confirmed, nothing for the garden species.
  - The authors say the premise (branches are kept or pruned according to carbon balance) is untested against water relations and other co-limits, and note some shade-intolerant pines transmit more than 25% light at high stocking.
- Schoonmaker et al. 2014 (*PLoS ONE* 9:e104187, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0104187) [PMC]: field trial on about 17-year-old lodgepole pine and white spruce, 3.3-4.5 m tall, 10 sites, two seasons of shading. Lower-crown light as a share of full light: unshaded 57.9%, whole tree shaded 10.8%, lower quarter-to-third shaded 7.1% (light) and 2.9% (heavy). **Correction:** the response measured was bud expansion and shoot growth, not branch death. In pine lower branches, bud expansion was about 40% under asymmetric shading, about 80% under uniform shading and 100% unshaded; shoot growth fell more than 50% under asymmetric shading. Spruce was affected less. Lower-branch needle carbohydrate in unshaded pine was 17% (spruce nearly 30%); asymmetric shading lowered lower-branch needle carbohydrate relative to the upper crown, read as reserves moving away or less being made (the authors favour less being made). Their conclusion is that the results support "a carbon based mechanism for branch mortality mediated by an asymmetry in light exposure", which is looser than the first pass's "hurt lower branches more". Juvenile conifers only.

**1.2 Branch autonomy is partial; branches compete with each other.**
- Henriksson 2001 (*Oecologia* 126:482-486, doi 10.1007/s004420000547) [abs]: mountain birch (*Betula pubescens* ssp. *czerepanovii*). After two summers 90% of individually shaded branches were dead; none died when the whole tree was shaded. Author's reading: fate depends on asymmetric competition among branches, not shade alone.
- Sprugel 2002 (*Tree Physiol.* 22:1119-1124, doi 10.1093/treephys/22.15-16.1119) [abs, PubMed abstract]: the abstract says correlative inhibition matters more than autonomy: branches on suppressed trees grow and make new foliage at irradiances where branches on dominant trees die, so "a positive carbon budget by itself does not ensure branch survival"; position relative to other branches matters. It generalises to other stresses. The first pass had this second-hand; the abstract confirms it. Schoonmaker's introduction [PMC] also cites Sprugel for the amabilis fir case (a fully shaded suppressed tree kept branches that a dominant would lose).
- Chen & Sumida 2017 (*Forests* 8:26) [snip]: in a Sakhalin spruce plantation, death probability of a whole primary branch fell the further it was above the crown base (p = 0.04) but was not significantly related to relative PPFD above it (p = 0.18). Chen et al. 2018 (*Trees*) [snip]: for primary branches, crown position matters as much as local light; for secondary branches light dominates. So light alone does not decide who dies.
- Majekobaje et al. 2023 (*Forests*) [snip]: in loblolly pine, a PCA of photosynthesis and water traits flagged branches that would die about four months before death was visible. Death is a slow decline, not a switch.

**1.3 Shade tolerance sets how deep the crown stays.**
- Kothari (above); Touzot et al. 2025 (*Oikos*) [snip]: shade-tolerant species have wider, deeper crowns and relative crown depth is less sensitive to local competition. This is the opposite of "tolerant species form a clear bole" (see section 5). Kothari's Lbase result agrees: tolerant, conservative species prune under deeper shade.

## 2. Death to fall: how long the dead branch stays

The model removes a shed branch at once. Real branches go through death, a standing dead stub, then fall, and the lag differs by species.

- **Kint, Hein, Campioli & Muys 2010** (*Forest Ecol. Manage.* 260:2023-2034, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2010.09.008) [full text, user copy]: 399 oak and 376 beech trees in 30 plots per species (circular, radius 4.5 m, around one chosen tree) in two Flemish forests, **young planted, even-aged stands (oak 15-22 y, beech 14-31 y; mean tree height about 10.8 and 10.4 m), branches counted only on the lowest 6 m of stem**. The 30 central trees per species (about 12 m tall) were picked as vital, (co)dominant quality trees, so the branch-status model is for good trees only. Findings, with the numbers:
  - **Death versus shedding.** Mean share of branches (lowest 6 m, from 8 mm diameter) that were dead: oak 0.79, beech 0.46 (Table 1, tree level). Of the branches and stumps measured on the central trees, 28% of oak and 71% of beech were alive. Authors' reading: similar total pruning rate, "early dying and slow shedding for oak and the other way around for beech", tied to oak's lower shade tolerance, slower wood decay and a different fungal flora. Beech's death and shedding are close in time, so its dead portion fluctuates and fits poorly (pseudo-R2 0.42, against 0.78 for oak).
  - **Branch mortality is an abrupt threshold in oak and gradual in beech** (Fig. 4, branch at 6 m height): a large oak branch is likely dead once the tree reaches about 16 m; a similar beech branch may still be alive at 20 m. The branch-status model (logit of being alive) gains with branch diameter (oak 0.203 per mm, beech 0.228 per mm) and with the branch's height as a percentage of tree height (0.195 and 0.089 per percentage point), and falls with tree height (-1.10 and -0.66 per m); oak also gains with ring width over the last five years (0.90 per mm). Cutpoint 0.30 (oak) and 0.74 (beech), AUC 0.97 and 0.88. So bigger and higher branches live; the authors read this as social position in the crown and carbon balance (Sprugel's autonomy).
  - **Density.** Oak self-pruning improves with stand density (plot volume, basal area, and LAI through the random plot effect). No such effect for beech, which the authors put down to a narrower range (about 4700 trees/ha for oak against about 2800 for beech at similar ages) and expect would appear at denser planting. They suggest contact with close neighbours speeds shedding, and that humid sites self-prune faster (rotting at the branch junction; no source found, flagged by them as needing confirmation).
  - **Lag in years: none.** The data are one cross-section of status per branch, so there is no time from death to fall. The paper's only statement on stubs is secondary: Butin & Kowalski 1983a report that oak branches thicker than 2 cm go step by step, dead branches first becoming stumps that "may take long time before shedding" (cited, not read). **Corrections to the earlier passes:** the abstract-level wording ("oak keeps the dead ones, beech drops them fast") stands and now has the 0.79 against 0.46 dead share behind it. The note had hoped the full text might hold lag years; it does not. The height of the first dead or first live branch is not reported; beech crown ratio is (mean 0.71 over all 376 trees, range 0-0.97, Table 1; none for oak), so the young beech clear-bole fraction is about 0.29, within trees of about 10 m.
- **Hein & Spiecker 2007** (*Can. J. For. Res.* 37:1414-1426, doi 10.1139/X06-308; ash and sycamore) [full text, user copy] and Hein 2008 (beech, 41 trees, 717 branches) [abs]. Method: stem dissection of 44 ash and 25 sycamore trees (635 and 334 branches; thinning-stage stands, dominant to subdominant trees only, rich sites, 57% planted), dating each branch's year of birth, year of death and year of complete occlusion by counting rings. Death is the year after the last ring joining stem and branch. **Occlusion time OT = year of full occlusion minus year of death**, so it starts at death and covers a stub that stands, rots or breaks, and is then overgrown. Numbers (natural pruning, Table 3): OT mean **7.7 y for ash and 6.9 y for sycamore, range 1-26 and 1-28 y**; branch diameter mean 14.9 and 12.6 mm (range 2-49 and 2-40 mm); radius of the embedded dead ("loose") knot portion mean 14.1 and 11.8 mm against a total occluded radius of 60 and 62 mm. OT rises with branch diameter and with dead-portion radius and falls with stem radial increment (R2 0.91; species not significant, so one pooled model). After artificial pruning of live branches OT is about 2.8 y (ash) and 3.8 y (sycamore), and the authors cite Zieren for 2-3 y for ash branches under 15 mm.
  - **What it says about stub fall.** The authors state that parts of a dead branch "seem to stay on the trunk until they become decomposed enough to slough off", that breakage is stochastic (snow, wind, crowns of thinned neighbours brushing the stem, fungal decay differing between plots), and that "natural pruning is therefore not sufficiently described by branch mortality". The relative dead portion of the knot falls with branch diameter but only 14% of its variance is explained; at a given diameter it is slightly larger in ash than sycamore. **Correction to the first pass:** it called these papers the closest source to "years from death to loss". They give death to overgrowth, not death to fall: OT is not an upper bound on the fall time (a stub need not fall to be occluded) and the fall is not dated. The embedded dead-knot radius (mean 12-14 mm) reflects how long a stub stood, but the paper gives no conversion to years.
  - **Crown base anchors (sampled trees, Table 2).** Self-pruned ash: height of lowest living branch 2.1-18.2 m, mean 11.4 m, tree height mean 20.8 m, crown ratio mean 45.7% (range 29.9-80.0); sycamore: lowest living branch mean 8.4 m (2.7-16.5), tree height 18.1 m, crown ratio 53.6% (34.2-82.2). That is a clear-bole fraction (1 - CR) of about 0.54 for ash and 0.46 for sycamore. Caveats: trees aged 12-79 (ash) and 18-123 (sycamore), no suppressed trees, 67% from mixed stands, sites chosen for quality timber.
  - The introduction lists three rules for broadleaves: open-grown trees have large crowns with short branch longevity; crown length shrinks under heavy competition; height growth and site affect clear-bole length.
- **Anatomical cladoptosis** (shedding at an abscission or brittle zone) exists for twigs and some branches: Schaffner & Tyler 1901 (*Ohio Naturalist* 1(3), via search summary) [snip] describe single-joint shedding in *Populus alba* and *Salix nigra* (clean cuts) and shed twigs 1-4 years old in *Quercus alba*. Old and weakly sourced; no modern review found [u].
- **Fall of dead stems** (the only quantified fall data found; branches are thinner, so their lag is shorter): almost all dead birch, pine and spruce stems fell within 40 years, birch fastest (Makinen et al. 2006, *Ecol. Appl.* [snip]); standing-dead time to fall falls with temperature and is longer for conifers (Gartner et al. 2023, *Forests* [snip]; Oberle et al. 2018, *PLoS ONE* [snip]); beech wood has the shortest residence time of Swiss species, 27-54 years (Hararuk et al. 2020, *Forest Ecosyst.* [snip]). **No primary source with branch-level years from death to fall was found, in any of four passes [u]**: the two full-text sources (Kint, Hein & Spiecker) do not date the fall. The nearest number is Hein & Spiecker's death-to-occlusion time of about 7 y (1-28) for ash and sycamore, which is a different event. The usable result is the ranking: broadleaf faster than conifer, warm faster than cold, beech fast, oak slow (Kint, now with the dead-share numbers above).
- Epicormic shoots complicate "bare": in pedunculate oak, new epicormic shoots on the lower stem (0-3 m) rise with stand density and after pruning (Attocchi 2013, *Ann. For. Sci.* [snip]).

## 3. Stand density and thinning

- Makela 1997 (*Forest Sci.* 43:7-24) [snip]: in a Scots pine carbon-balance model the pruning height follows a self-pruning coefficient made a function of crown coverage; lower stocking gives larger crown ratios.
- Beech: Reventlow et al. 2018 (*Forestry*) [snip]: the lowest live branch sits lower with heavy thinning. The related Danish study (Zhu 2005 thesis summary [snip]): "natural pruning was hampered by increasing thinning grade".
- Oak, measured: Kint 2010 [full text, user copy] finds oak's dead branch share and branch count respond to stand volume, basal area and LAI; beech showed no density effect over its narrower density range (section 2).
- Oak: young stands are kept "quite dense for good natural pruning", and a beech, hornbeam and lime understorey "promotes the natural shedding of lower branches of the oak" (Nicolescu et al. 2025, *J. For. Res.* [snip]).
- Birch plantations: natural pruning rate rises with planting density, most abscission in the leaf-fall season (Wang et al. 2026, *Trees For. People*, *Betula alnoides* [snip]).

## 4. Side light: edges and gaps

Evidence is observational; a direct experiment on lateral light and branch survival was not found [u]. The first pass had no authors or journals for these; they are now captured.
- **Seidel, Ruzicka & Puettmann 2016**, *Forest Ecol. Manage.* 363:31-38, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2015.12.024 [repo: Oregon State Andrews Forest PDF, full text]. Douglas-fir plantation in the western Cascades, thinned to 275 trees/ha with 0.2 ha artificial gaps; terrestrial laser scanning of 20 border trees and 20 interior trees 18 years after gap creation. Table 1 (border vs interior): crown base height 9.83 vs 15.59 m (p < 0.001); live crown ratio 73.7% vs 59.0%; crown length 27.5 vs 22.5 m; crown asymmetry directed to the gap, strongest in the lowest third of the crown. The authors conclude border trees have shorter branch-free boles and larger knots. This is the one quantified side-light case: a conifer with a 6 m crown-base difference. They note earlier work found similar asymmetry in maple, birch, ash, oak and European beech (Muth & Bazzaz 2003; Seidel et al. 2011b), not read.
- **Sherich, Pocewicz & Morgan 2007**, *Can. J. For. Res.* 37:2096-2105, doi 10.1139/X07-105 [full text, user copy]: 23 forest edges older than 50 years in northern Idaho (diffuse, fairly open edges bordering fields and meadows; edge trees within 10 m of the drip line, interior trees 50-75 m in), 192 canopy-dominant or codominant ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir. Crown ratio (live crown depth over tree height, from the height to the live crown base; two base heights taken when the crown was uneven): pine 0.69 at the edge against 0.59 inside, Douglas-fir 0.71 against 0.58 (Table 2; edge effect p < 0.01). On edge trees the crown ratio is much higher on the edge-facing (open) side than the forest-facing side, about 0.79 against 0.61 from figure (Fig. 2; p < 0.01), a gap of about 0.18 of tree height; interior trees show no such side difference (about 0.595 and 0.58 from figure). Leaf area was 17% (pine, 116 against 100 m2) and 42% (Douglas-fir, 252 against 178 m2) larger at edges, and basal area growth was higher. Stand density index, tree number and DBH did not differ between edge and interior, so the deeper crowns are a light effect, not a density one. The authors cite Muth & Bazzaz 2003 (not read): trees answer uneven light mainly by differential growth and survival of branches, not by leaning; and Wardman & Schmidt 1998 (not read): Douglas-fir at gap edges had crowns 62% wider and 36% deeper than under closed canopy. Conifers on a dry site; the paper measures live crown depth, not dead-branch timing. This replaces the first pass's "uief_0189" citation. Nothing in the abstract-level reading was contradicted.
- **Salek, Zahradnik, Marusak, Jerabkova & Merganic 2013**, *Forest Ecol. Manage.* 305:1-10, doi 10.1016/j.foreco.2013.05.012 [full text, user copy]: managed riparian forests (ash 30%, oak 27%, lime 17% by area) near Kromeriz, Czech Republic; 50 plots of 10 x 50 m running from 1 m outside the edge to 49 m in; 1159 trees of 19 species with DBH above 7 cm (ash 45%, lime 24%, oak 13%, sycamore 7%), analysed pooled, not by species. Edge-to-interior breakpoints from piecewise regression: standing volume 4 m, basal area 4 m, tree number 6 m, straight trees 6 m, crown length ratio **8 m**, quality-A stems (long branch-free stem, no visible defect) 10 m, slenderness 18 m; the overall breakpoint, an average over eight variables, is 8 m (range 4-18). **Height of the first green branch was measured but is not reported by distance**; only the derived crown length ratio is. The crown effect is small: magnitude of edge influence 0.047 (R2 0.48; on (e - i)/(e + i) that is about 10% more crown at the edge; the sign is taken from the text, which counts crown length among the positive edge effects). The quality-A share is lower near the edge (magnitude 0.23). The authors say the crown ratio breakpoint lies close to the edge because these edges are much denser than the interior and "light from side did not penetrate deeply"; they cite Evers & Didham 2006 for a side-light depth of about 5 m (not read). **Correction (first and second pass):** the paper never gave a first-green-branch result for the edge gradient, and its crown effect is weak. The edge width of 4-18 m stands, but it is a stand-structure result. The weak crown effect is evidence of a short side-light reach in dense edges, the opposite of Sherich's open-edge result.
- The sand pine edge study cited in the first pass could not be re-found (a targeted search returned only species descriptions); drop it unless located. The gap-border claim in the first pass is now Seidel 2016.
- Reading Sherich, Salek and Seidel together: the crown-base effect is large where the edge is open (Seidel, a gap: crown base 5.8 m lower on border trees; Sherich, an open edge: crown ratio about 0.18 higher on the lit side) and small where the edge is dense and the light enters only a few metres (Salek: 8 m breakpoint, about 10% more crown). Side light matters in proportion to how far it reaches into the crown. Direct numbers for broadleaves under side light are still absent (Salek is mixed broadleaves but pooled and weak).
- Mechanism: Henriksson, Sprugel and Schoonmaker say a branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours, so a limb lit from the side is not in deficit even if the sky above is blocked. Kothari's Lbase is a mostly vertical reading and cannot separate this. Seidel's numbers support the direction (lower crown base on the lit side), not the mechanism.

Caution on "shade-tolerant means clear bole": the data say the live crown of tolerant species stays deeper (Kothari, Touzot). Beech clears its bole in dense stands because it is dense and because its dead limbs drop quickly; oak clears with help from companions.

## Implications for the model

**Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence.**
1. **A branch dies when it is darker than its neighbours and the tree can do without it (rank, not absolute light).** Strongest: Henriksson (90% vs 0%, abstract), Sprugel (abstract), Kothari's Ltop effect (full text, p = 0.002, but confounded by neighbour death and young trees), Schoonmaker (full text, but juvenile conifers and bud/shoot response, not death). C5's T_rel (ADR 0007) is relative, but to the open sky. This evidence says the threshold should rise with the tree's own top light, which is what ADR 0043's vigour gate reached for without a source. It is a cited alternative to Tvig, not a licence to bring Tvig back; it is a C5 change and needs the user.
2. **Species threshold set by leaf life and shade tolerance (Lbase).** Strong, quantified (full text), but only as per-species means in a figure, in a different unit from the model's Q, from a 9-year-old plantation, and silent on European species. The measure is the live-crown base, so it fits the model's visible bole height but not the dead-stub lag. Beech's T_rel of 0.008 is a shade-leaf figure (Hagemeier & Leuschner, per ADR 0043); Lbase is a branch-death figure. They are different measures and may differ a lot. Test before blaming C5.
3. **Death-to-fall lag, species-specific.** Moderate for the direction, weak for the size. Kint (full text) shows oak with 79% dead branches in the lowest 6 m against beech's 46%, and an abrupt oak threshold against beech's gradual one, in young plantations only. Hein & Spiecker (full text) show dead branches standing and decaying until they break, at stochastic times, with death to overgrowth taking about 7 y (1-28) in ash and sycamore; no source dates the fall itself. The model has no dead-stub state, so shedding is instant. A lag would leave oak's dead stubs visible longer and beech's bole clean; for ash and sycamore a lag of several seasons is plausible but unmeasured.
4. **Stand density, via crown coverage.** Moderate (Makela, Reventlow, Nicolescu). Density already acts through light in the model, so this is a check, not a rule: does the lower crown see less light as the stand closes?
5. **Branch position or distance to crown base.** Weak to moderate (Chen & Sumida); probably a proxy for light history.
6. **Anatomical cladoptosis.** Weak (old sources, poplar and willow). Not a candidate for the garden species.

**Role of side light.** A frontal slab with a vertical light sweep may credit a lower limb with side light from the slab edge or from gaps between neighbours. Real edge and gap trees keep lower limbs, and the rank rule says why: a sideways-lit branch is not darker than its neighbours. If lower limbs survive in the model's stands, side light is a likelier cause than the threshold. The only quantified case is Seidel 2016 (full text): Douglas-fir gap-border trees had a crown base 5.8 m lower than interior trees (9.8 vs 15.6 m), with the lower crown skewed to the gap. It is a conifer, 18 years after gap creation, 20 trees per group. Sherich 2007 (full text) adds an open conifer edge: crown ratio about 0.79 on the lit side against 0.61 on the shaded side (from figure). Salek 2013 (full text) adds a dense mixed-broadleaf edge where the crown effect is small (about 10%, 8 m breakpoint) because side light reaches only about 5 m. So the credit a lower limb gets from the side should depend on how far the light reaches into the crown: a lateral term with a short reach, not a free pass from the slab edge.

**Diagnostics to run before changing any rule** (all are measurements):
1. For each stand tree at run end, plot the light per metamer (C5's measure) of the lowest living limb against the species' T and T_rel. ADR 0043 found the lone beech's limbs 14-80x above threshold; repeat for stand trees.
2. Split that light into vertical and lateral parts: how much of a surviving lower limb's light enters from the side (slab edge, neighbours' gaps)? If most, side light is the cause.
3. Compare the model's clear-bole fraction (lowest living limb height over tree height) with 1 - CR: beech 0.34-0.43, oak about 0.58, over at least 20 seeds with the pass rate stated (AGENTS.md).
4. Measure crown-base height over tree height in the Wytham Woods TLS data named in the [reference-data note](2026-11-03-reference-data-and-evaluation.md) (sycamore, oak, ash, hazel) to get real clear-bole fractions for three garden species from full data.
5. A switch test: shed instantly versus a fall lag of n seasons for dead limbs, to see whether visible bole height changes (it should for oak, little for beech). Sweep n up to about 8, since death to occlusion in ash and sycamore averages about 7 y; this is a sweep range, not a measured fall time.
6. Before any rank-based shedding code, analyse recorded runs: which limbs below the tree's own median limb light would have been shed?

**Open gaps** (candidates for the map): branch-level death-to-fall years for beech, oak, ash, hornbeam (still none after the fourth pass; Hein 2008 for beech, abstract only, is the remaining occlusion source); holly and hornbeam branch data (none); a direct lateral-light experiment; per-species Lbase values (Kothari Figure 2 is a plot; the supplement and data repository were not opened, and the authors may supply the numbers); an Lbase-like measure for European broadleaves.

**What the second pass changed in these conclusions:** none of the ranking moved. Three claims were narrowed: Kint is young plantations to 6 m, Schoonmaker measured bud and shoot growth on juvenile conifers, and Kothari's height effect depends on species. The Czech edge study is not crown evidence.

**What the fourth pass (user-supplied PDFs) changed:** the ranking did not move. Kint's oak-versus-beech contrast now has numbers, but the full text has no lag in years. Hein & Spiecker's occlusion time is death to overgrowth (mean about 7 y, 1-28), not death to fall, so the lag stays unmeasured. New anchors: clear-bole fraction about 0.54 (ash) and 0.46 (sycamore); side light raises crown ratio by about 0.18 on the lit side at an open conifer edge (Sherich) but only about 10% at a dense broadleaf edge (Salek).
