## 1. Mechanisms, with sources

### 1.1 Loss of apical control with age and size
- Wilson (2000), *Am. J. Bot.* 87(5): 601-607, DOI 10.2307/2656846, "Apical control of branch growth and angle in woody plants" [full text, user copy]: a review, not a field study of crowns. Apical control is inhibition of a lateral by the shoots above it. It begins once lateral buds have broken and leafed; the uppermost lateral becomes longest and the lowest scarcely elongates; it **also limits older, large branches**, and inhibits radial growth as well as elongation. Removing the upper shoots (cutting, or a phloem girdle) lets a branch grow larger and often turn upward, and the branch often replaces the injured leader and **re-imposes apical control**, so the characteristic branching pattern is maintained. Mechanism: the candidates are hormones and assimilate allocation (the stem acting as a competing sink for the branch's assimilate, so a branch can be limited by assimilate even with adequate hormone); the paper rules out hydraulic bottleneck and gravimorphism as the *primary* cause (their changes are too slow after release) and concludes there is probably no single mechanism. The hormonal role is "not known how".
- What the full text adds for the rounding question: (a) apical control is **weaker in slow-growing plants**: reduced in shaded conifers, in higher-order slower branches and in old slow-growing trees (Moorby & Wareing 1963, cited, not read), and **well-lit branches partly escape it**. That supports the sign of the leader-slowing story in the reading above, but only as a cited statement, with no age or size numbers. (b) Release **restores** a leader (takeover), so it rebuilds the excurrent pattern; a dome would need the takeover repeated or the replacement to stay oblique, which the paper does not address. (c) The experiments are mostly *Pinus strobus* and other young conifers; for angiosperms it reports only that tension wood formed and branches bent up after decapitation in *Prunus serotina* and *Fraxinus americana* (Wilson & Archer 1983, cited). Nothing in it concerns crown outline or age at onset.
- Reading (ours): a leader that slows (1.2) or dies releases the laterals beneath it; the strongest takes over as a new leader (sympodial takeover, already row 5 of the simulators note's gap table). This is the generative step of a dome. The broadleaf sources for it are the Troll-model and reiteration papers already reviewed.
- Architectural-model classification: **beech Troll, maple Rauh is not supported by the full text** of Barthélémy & Caraglio (2007), *Ann. Bot.* 99: 375-407 [PMC2802949, read]. What it does say: Rauh's model has rhythmic growth, all axes monopodial, lateral sexuality; Acer sp. is drawn as a monopodial system and Ulmus sp. and Platanus as sympodial (after Troll 1937); sympodial successions can mimic a monopodial axis ("pseudomonopodium"), so an outline cannot be read back to a model. The beech-Troll and maple-Rauh labels stay [mem]. The same paper says gradual architectural change with development stage in *Fagus sylvatica* was described by Nicolini (1997) and Nicolini & Chanson (1999) (its Fig. 27), which are the stage sequences to find. Hallé, Oldeman & Tomlinson (1978) and Oldeman (1974) introduced the models and reiteration [abs, bibliographic only].

### 1.2 Hydraulic and height limits
- Ryan & Yoder (1997), *BioScience* 47(4): 235-242, DOI 10.2307/1313077 [full text, user copy; a scan, read as page images]: a review of four explanations for the slowing of height growth with age (respiration, nutrient limitation, maturation of the meristem, hydraulic limitation); it judges hydraulic limitation "the most promising" and examines it in detail. The argument: resistance to water flow rises with path length (tree height and branch length) and as wood permeability falls; stomata close earlier to avoid cavitation; assimilation falls; wood production (low priority for carbon) falls, which makes later xylem narrower and the resistance higher (a negative feedback). Anchors: *Eucalyptus regnans* grows 2-3 m per year young, 50 cm per year at 90 years and almost not at all by 150; ponderosa pine reaches 50 m on a good site and 10 m on a poor one, and two 250-year-old trees in the paper's Fig. 1 are 37 m and 7 m; the gravity gradient is about 0.01 MPa per m, with a steeper gradient of 0.024 MPa per m cited for tall trees; Scots pine whole-tree conductance at 40 years is about 20% of the seedling value; canopy conductance in 30 m, 270-year ponderosa is 50% lower than in 10 m, 40-year trees; diurnal photosynthesis in 32 m ponderosa is 25% lower than in 10 m trees; spruce transpiration at 140 years is about 20% below that at 40. Caveats the paper states itself: the evidence for increased resistance in older trees is "not conclusive" (a eucalypt study found no height gradient in water potential, and the pressure-probe data are suspect); the support is mainly conifers, with *Betula occidentalis* branch notching and cherry photosynthesis the broadleaf data; and fertilizing a 120-year Scots pine restarted height growth, so the limit can be moved by resources. Ryan, Phillips & Bond (2006) revisit: hydraulic limitation of gas exchange with size is common, not universal [sec].
- **What the paper says about form, which differs from the first reading.** It opens with the observation that as trees stop growing taller, the treetop becomes flattened, primary branches thickened and smaller branches twisted and gnarled because the annual extension of *all* branches is also reduced; and it argues that the new-growth limit should be most severe where hydraulic resistance is greatest: the treetop, the tips of very long branches and the tips of twigs, so that "the flattened tops and gnarled branches of old trees" are an expected result. So the limit applies to **every** shoot with a long path to the root, laterals included. Our earlier inference (a leader cap while laterals keep their supply, so W/H rises) is not supported, and the paper's own form prediction is a flattened top with thickened limbs. The paper is conifer-heavy and gives no outline measurement, W/H or broadleaf crown data. It also notes that isolated old trees in parks with ample water and nutrients do not keep growing in height.
- Consequence for shape (our inference only, now within the paper's constraint): a cap on **total path length from the root** would limit the leader and the long lateral tips alike, so the envelope would follow a surface of roughly constant path length, which is a rounded top over a spreading crown, not a leader cap with free laterals. Whether it gives a dome or a flat cap depends on how steeply extension falls near the cap; the paper gives no number. Sterck & Schieving (2007), "3-D growth patterns of trees", *Ecol. Monogr.* 77(3): 405-420, DOI 10.1890/06-1670.1 [abs; Wageningen repository record, full text not obtained], a 3-D functional-structural model, report a sigmoid expansion phase and a stable steady-state phase, limits to tree size including height, and constant allometric scaling in the expansion phase, all emerging from selection for net carbon gain. Their assumptions include new meristems placed where branches fall (so a viable meristem population stays inside the crown) and metamers placed where carbon benefit over a leaf pipe's life is greatest. The abstract does not describe the crown outline. They are the closest published model analogue to this project; the full text should say whether crowns round in the steady-state phase.
- Koch et al. (2004), *Nature* 428: 851-854, DOI 10.1038/nature02417, "The limits to tree height" [full text, user copy]: measured in *Sequoia sempervirens* up to 112.7 m at Humboldt Redwoods State Park (a conifer at 100+ m, far from the model's species and sizes). Predawn xylem pressure falls 0.0096 MPa per m of height (five trees over 110 m), nearly the gravity gradient (0.0098); nearly two-thirds of midday xylem pressure is due to gravity; the lowest value, about -1.84 MPa at 108 m, is close to the pressure at which loss of conductivity begins (-1.9 MPa). Turgor falls linearly with height (0.93 MPa at 50 m to 0.48 MPa at 110 m); leaf mass per area rises exponentially (a fourfold range); photosynthesis per leaf mass at 110 m is 28% of that at 80 m and 16% of that at 50 m, while photosynthesis per leaf *area* did not vary with height. Regressions on four variables give a maximum height of 122-130 m; the tallest trees grow by up to 0.25 m per year. The paper concerns **leaf** water status and gives no branch-extension or crown-width data; its crown-shape relevance is in 1.7 (multiple tops).

### 1.3 Crown development phases (empirical sequence)
- Roloff's four phases are exploration, degeneration, stagnation and resignation. Innes (1998), *Forestry* 71(2): 113-130, DOI 10.1093/forestry/71.2.113 [full text, user copy] reviews them for **beech (*Fagus sylvatica*)**. They were defined by Roloff (1984-1989) on the **uppermost 2 m of the crown**, equated to "without", "light", "medium" and "heavy" damage, and read as vitality classes at all ages; trees older than about 140-160 years are usually excluded because the sequence also happens naturally with old age. The classes "probably reflect the progressive deterioration of the crown"; scores depend on light, competition, genetics, flowering and site, and many trees fall between classes.
  - **Phase definitions and shoot-length criteria (Roloff, as reported by Innes):**

    | Phase | Apical (leader) growth | Crown and twig description | Age in healthy stands |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Exploration | above 30 cm per year on suitable sites | vigorous ramification in the upper crown; terminal and upper laterals form long shoots, lower laterals short shoots; most crown space used; **crown has a rounded appearance** | up to about 140 years |
    | Degeneration | reduced to about 20 cm per year | side shoots no longer branch ("spear-like" twigs); terminal still forms a long shoot, shorter than in a young tree; gaps; **spiky** crown | after about 150 years |
    | Stagnation | a few centimetres per year | no or very short side twigs on the main axes; terminal bud forms a short shoot; **brush-like** appearance, "claw-like" twigs oriented upward | later |
    | Resignation | under 1 cm per year, or stagnation for several years | side twigs break off or fail to develop; main axis may break; dieback; fragmented crown | late |

    Other numbers in the paper: long shoots up to 75 cm in young trees but generally 10-20 cm in trees of 60-150 years; short shoots under 5 cm, carrying about 75% of the foliage, while long shoots make about 60% of the annual dry-weight increment (Renard 1971); normal upper-crown long shoots 10-75 cm per year; healthy mature trees 20-30 cm per year before drought, with dominant trees under 70 years at 25-30 and older trees at about 15 (Lonsdale et al. 1989); a **threshold of about 20 cm per year** (Woodcock et al. 1995), below which the number of branches per unit length falls and **branching angles become more acute**, followed, in their proposed decline sequence, by crown thinning, bark scald and disintegration. For saplings under 3 m, Stribley's scale scores previous-year shoots 0 for over 15 cm, 1 for 10-15 and 2 for under 10. Age effects: in trees under 80 years in north-east Switzerland about 70% were class 0, under 35% above 120 years; architecture scores rose to about 120 years, then stabilized (Ling et al. 1993).
  - **Not in the paper:** any outline measurement, crown width, or W/H against age. The phases describe twig and leader structure in the top 2 m.
- Reading (ours): the full text corrects the second pass in one respect. The exploration phase is the one called **rounded**, and it is the vigorous one (leader above 30 cm per year). The phases that follow, with the leader slowing below about 20 cm per year, are spiky, brush-like and fragmented, with more acute branch angles (upward, not outward). So Roloff's sequence is a decline of the *top 2 m*, not a documented route to a dome, and in beech a slowing leader is associated with loss of fullness in the upper crown, not with lateral takeover. Upper-crown shoot length is still the measurable quantity behind the phases and the same signal as the leader/lateral extension ratio in 5.2, with beech anchors above (20 cm per year as the change-point).

### 1.4 Light geometry: side light and flank loading
- Horn (1971), *The Adaptive Geometry of Trees*, Princeton: multilayer crowns suit full sun, monolayer crowns suit shade [sec]. Kuuluvainen (1992), *Oikos* 65: 275-284 [abs, partial]: sun elevation by latitude favours narrow crowns at high latitude and wider, flatter or domed crowns at low latitude; a later modelling study finds the latitude effect weaker than claimed, working mainly through site productivity [sec].
- Reading (ours): for a lone tree, light is highest at the top and flanks, so the outer shell is the productive surface; an envelope that maximises exposed shell for a given foliage volume is round. In stands the flank is shaded and the same rule gives a tall narrow crown (the stand's lid, ADR 0031). No source found tests flank light as the specific cause of rounding.

### 1.5 Branch reorientation and arching
- Wilson (2000) above [full text, user copy]: branch angle is itself under apical control; released branches turn up. Its mechanics section treats a branch as a cantilever beam (Niklas 1992, cited, not read): a branch at an angle to vertical always carries a downward bending moment from its own mass, and it can bend up only if new wood generates enough upward moment through differential growth stress (tension wood in most angiosperms). Some branches, including plagiotropic ones with a fixed equilibrium angle, never bend to vertical, and some shrubs cannot bend up at all; in *Pseudotsuga* and *Pinus strobus* the equilibrium angle can change slowly after release. So the ingredients of arching (self-load against growth-stress moment) are sourced, from a review and mainly from conifers; no measurement of tip arching or limb angle against age in the model's broadleaves. Together these give the "ascending co-dominant limbs that arch at the tips" ADR 0031 names. Innes 1998 (1.3) adds that more acute branching angles accompany a leader below about 20 cm per year in beech, which points the other way for the upper crown.

### 1.6 Reiteration and re-crowning
- Nicolini, Chanson & Bonne (2001), *Ann. Bot.* 87(6): 737-750, DOI 10.1006/anbo.2001.1398 [abs; closed]: suppressed understorey beech are re-crowned by epicormic branches as the canopy closes; stem cambial activity drops sharply with the new epicormic crown and then girth growth resumes. This is a **suppressed-tree** route, not the open-grown one. A GreenLab beech paper [arXiv, Letort et al. 2008, searched in full] cites it for epicormics that replace a deficient crown, observed on one of its two understorey trees but not modelled. Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007 [PMC] separate **adaptive reiteration** (a response to increased resources) from **traumatic** reiteration (after loss of structure) and **sequential** (automatic) reiteration, which occurs after a definite threshold of differentiation and is "a common feature of tree development and crown construction". Sequential reiteration is thus an age-programmed route to a multi-leader crown, the nearest sourced analogue of a rounding trigger.
- Nicolini (1997) and Nicolini & Chanson (1999) give stage-by-stage architecture of *Fagus sylvatica* (cited in Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007, Fig. 27 [PMC]); originals not found open. Colin et al. 2012, *Ann. Bot.* 110: 995-1005 [PMC, read] is on beech trunk epicormics (they sprout less where radial growth is high) and has nothing on outline.

### 1.7 Leader dieback
- Oak and hornbeam outlines in species-identity already carry "stag-headed" and "domed" wording (TSO, JRC). Koch et al. (2004) [full text, user copy] give the first direct observation in the set: "nearly all very tall redwoods have multiple tops, the original leader having died and been replaced repeatedly". The authors suggest this may happen in dry episodes when upper-crown xylem tension passes the cavitation threshold (a suggestion; no dieback dates or counts are given). It is a 100+ m conifer, so it shows the route exists under a hydraulic limit, not that broadleaves round this way or at what age. Ryan & Yoder (1997) add that tops flatten and large branches thicken as growth stalls. Timing of dieback in oak, beech or hornbeam is still unmeasured here.

## 2. Which species round, which stay conical

Evidence found is **descriptive**, mostly already in species-identity. New here: the architecture-model split in 1.1 (Troll for beech, Rauh for maple [sec]) and Millet et al. 1999 (already cited elsewhere): birch and aspen have almost no total reiteration, so stay excurrent with a narrow aperture; maple and beech use total reiteration, which "promotes lateral spread". Conifer pyramids are kept by strong apical control until old age; for fir and spruce the existing docs record cone to dome with age.

Not found: onset age or height for oak, maple, beech, hornbeam. **Lime, one weak anchor:** Martynova (2020), *SEEFOR* 11(1): 51-59, DOI 10.15177/seefor.20-04 [OA, read], measured small-leaved lime (*Tilia cordata*) in Ufa, Russia, twice, 11 years apart. In a park stand (stand density 1.0; n = 626 then 623): height 18.2 to 20.8 m, DBH 18.4 to 19.7 cm, crown diameter 6.0 to 9.4 m, so W/H rose from 0.33 to 0.45 while height rose 14%. Along a pruned street alley (n = 727 then 688): height 13.1 to 14.0 m, DBH 30.1 to 34.1 cm, crown diameter 9.0 to 10.2 m, W/H 0.69 to 0.73. The paper reports crown length falling from 8.5 to 3.67 m in the park, which sits oddly with the widening crown and is not explained in the text, so treat it as a definition or measurement quirk. The data are plot means, not individual trees followed through life, from managed urban plantings; they show crown width rising faster than height in mature lime and nothing about onset.

## 3. Open-grown versus stand-grown
- Pretzsch et al. (2015), *Urban For. Urban Green.* 14: 466-479, DOI 10.1016/j.ufug.2015.04.006 [OA, read in full from the user's copy]. 39,057 trees of 22 species, mostly south German long-term plots plus nine cities; ages about 20 to over 200 years; pruned solitary trees excluded where possible.
  - **Method:** quantile regression of ln(cr) = a + α·ln(d) (crown radius cr in m, d = DBH in cm). The **95% quantile stands for open-grown trees** (widest crown at a given d), 50% for average and 5% for suppressed. Height uses the **5% quantile** of ln(h) = a + α·ln(d) for open-grown trees (lowest height at a given d).
  - **Crown-extension types** (k-means on cr25 and α): type 1 "large crown, moderate slope" = *Tilia cordata*, *Carpinus betulus*, *Fagus sylvatica*, *Ulmus glabra*, *Aesculus*, *Platanus*, *Robinia*, *Khaya* (only broadleaves); type 2 "medium, medium slope" = *Betula pendula*, *Pinus sylvestris*, *Fraxinus excelsior*, *Larix*, *Alnus*; type 3 "medium, steep slope" = *Quercus robur*, *Picea glauca*, *Pseudotsuga*; type 4 "medium, flat slope" = *Acer pseudoplatanus*, *Sorbus aucuparia*, *Picea abies*, *Abies alba*. Types do not follow phylogeny.
  - **The crown-radius exponent** α_cr ranges from 0.39 to 0.83 and clusters near 2/3, but oak's (0.816) is among the steepest and beech's (0.471) among the flattest: an oak keeps widening its crown with girth, while a beech widens it early and then slows.

  - **Derived W/H = 2·cr/h** (my arithmetic from the table, not a figure in the paper): oak 1.11 at d 25 → 1.21 at d 60; lime 0.83 → 1.15; birch 0.52 → 0.57; sycamore 0.41 → 0.39; hornbeam 0.57 → 0.49; beech 0.72 → 0.51; rowan 0.39 → 0.30; pine 0.39 → 0.30; silver fir 0.32 → 0.25. **Caveats:** cr and h come from opposite quantiles, so this is an envelope (widest crown over shortest stem), not one tree's shape, and it overstates W/H. Oak's height fit rests on 69 trees and its h25 of 5.9 m looks low (urban Cape Town trees are in the sample). Sycamore and rowan heights are from forest plots. Read the trends, not the levels.
  - **No time axis.** The authors say the relationships give crown size at a given diameter, not when it is reached; diameter growth is too variable to build general age–diameter curves. So the paper calibrates shape against **girth**, not against age.
  - **What it means for rounding:** only oak and lime (and, weakly, birch) widen faster than they grow tall as girth increases (α_cr > α_h). Beech, hornbeam, sycamore, rowan and the conifers get *relatively narrower* with size when open-grown. If "rounding" means W/H rising with size, this dataset supports it for oak and lime only; beech and hornbeam keep broad crowns (type 1, the largest cr25) without W/H rising. A dome for beech and hornbeam would therefore be about **outline shape** (fullness of the top), not W/H.

Trees with severely deformed or asymmetric crowns were rejected (rarely). For ash and beech a single tree's crown diameter has a prediction interval of at least 20% of the value; poplar and walnut are much tighter. Earlier work covered only oak (Jobling & Pearce 1977).

    | *Sorbus aucuparia* | 13 | 1.096 | 18.45 | 93.2 | 23.9 | 22.1 | no data above 0.4 m (21.2) |

    Hornbeam is **not** in the paper (the species are sycamore, birch, sweet chestnut, beech, ash, walnut, poplar, cherry, oak, rowan and lime). Rowan rests on 13 trees. The lime and chestnut lines are from Savill 1991, walnut from Hemery 2000 and cherry from Pryor 1985, so they are not new data of this study.
  - **Trend in K/d:** every species has a higher K/d when young (24 to 46 at 0.1 m) and it falls with diameter, by about 60% from 0.10 to 0.70 m, **beginning to stabilise around 30-40 cm**; the paper says the ratio "diminishes with age, but never increases". The authors note the true relation may be sigmoid and may be depressed at the top end by senility, but treat the line as adequate for 0.2-0.5 m. Light demand does not predict the ratio (the shade-tolerant lime, chestnut and beech have the lowest K/d at 0.6 m, but the paper stresses this is the opposite of North American findings that tolerant species have larger crowns); lime and chestnut have strikingly flat slopes.
  - **Comparison with Pretzsch 2015** (my arithmetic; Pretzsch's crown diameter is 2·cr at the 95% quantile, an open-grown upper edge, while Hemery's line is a woodland mean):

    | Species | d = 25 cm: Hemery K / Pretzsch 2cr (m) | ratio | d = 60 cm: Hemery K / Pretzsch 2cr (m) | ratio |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Oak | 5.6 / 6.6 | 0.86 | 11.1 / 13.4 | 0.83 |
    | Beech | 4.9 / 9.2 | 0.54 | 10.3 / 13.9 | 0.74 |
    | Ash | 5.8 / 6.2 | 0.94 | 13.0 / 10.6 | 1.22 |
    | Sycamore | 5.4 / 7.1 | 0.75 | 11.9 / 11.4 | 1.04 |
    | Birch | 5.0 / 6.1 | 0.82 | 10.7 / 10.8 | 0.99 |
    | Lime | 5.1 / 9.0 | 0.57 | 9.1 / 14.9 | 0.61 |

    The expected pattern holds where the datasets agree: woodland crowns are narrower than the open-grown upper edge, most strongly for beech and lime (0.54-0.74), the two species with the largest cr25 in Pretzsch; oak is 0.83-0.86 at both sizes. **Where they disagree:** at 60 cm Hemery's ash, sycamore and birch lines sit at or above Pretzsch's 95% line (1.22, 1.04, 0.99). A woodland mean should not exceed an open-grown upper quantile, so for these species at large d at least one extrapolation is off (Hemery's line is straight, Pretzsch's is a power law, and Hemery notes the difficulty of finding enough large trees). Read the 25 cm column as the more reliable one.
  - **Local exponent (crown width against diameter):** the elasticity b·d/(a+b·d) of Hemery's line is 0.69 (oak), 0.77 (beech), 0.87 (ash), 0.87 (sycamore), 0.81 (birch) and 0.56 (lime) at 25 cm, rising to 0.85, 0.89, 0.94, 0.94, 0.91 and 0.75 at 60 cm. These exceed Pretzsch's open-grown exponents for all but oak (beech 0.471, ash 0.605, sycamore 0.534, birch 0.648, lime 0.573; oak 0.816). So in woodland, beech and sycamore crowns widen almost in proportion to stem diameter, whereas the open-grown envelope widens more slowly; the datasets are different statistics (a mean line against a 95% quantile fit) and different growing conditions. Pretzsch's beech statement ("widens early, then slows") is not contradicted but is not seen in Hemery's stand-grown line. Hemery has no height data, so W/H cannot be formed from this paper.
  - **Relevance to rounding:** the Pretzsch envelope is the target for a lone (open-grown) model tree; Hemery's lines are a lower, stand-grown reference. The paper offers no outline, age or height information.
- Pretzsch (2014), *For. Ecol. Manage.* 327: 251-264 [abs]: mixed canopies fill space more densely and plastically; crown-projection sums are higher in mixtures. Bears on the stand case, not on rounding itself.
- Reading (ours): open-grown crowns are limited by the tree's own supply, so the hydraulic and apical-control routes dominate; stand-grown crowns are limited by neighbours' light and stay narrow, with recession from below (ADR 0031's lid). Beech's long clean bole in stands and low massive limbs open-grown is in species-identity.

## 4. What is still not known
- The age or height at which any named species rounds, in any units. (The Roloff phases give beech leader-growth thresholds of about 30, 20 and a few cm per year for the top 2 m, and ages of about 140 and 150 years for the first two phases, but describe the exploration phase as the rounded one.)
- A published W/H trajectory (age on x) for any species.
- A direct test of side light or arching as a cause.
- Whether Roloff's phases correspond to a particular outline change: Innes 1998 describes them as a decline scale of the top 2 m, with the first (vigorous) phase rounded; the change in whole-crown outline with age is not measured there.
- Whether the hydraulic limit acts on lateral shoots in broadleaves as Ryan & Yoder argue (branch tips limited by path length), and what envelope results; the only direct data are conifers (and *Sequoia* leaf water status, Koch 2004).
- Hemery 2005 and Pretzsch 2015 disagree at large diameter for ash, sycamore and birch; neither paper settles which is right.
- Which architectural model beech and maple follow (the first pass's Troll/Rauh claim failed its check).

## 5. Implications for the model

### 5.1 Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence for rounding the open-grown broadleaf crown
(Order revised in the fourth pass. The previous order was: 1 apical control and takeover, 2 hydraulic limit on the leader, 3 reiteration, 4 arching, 5 flank light, 6 leader dieback.)

1. **A path-length (hydraulic) limit on shoot extension, applied to the leader and to long lateral tips alike** (1.2). Now the best-sourced mechanism for why growth stalls at the top and the outline changes: Ryan & Yoder 1997 [full text, user copy] give the argument, measured conductance and photosynthesis reductions in tall conifers, and the explicit prediction of flattened tops with thick gnarled limbs; Koch 2004 [full text, user copy] gives the measured water-potential gradient (0.0096 MPa per m, near gravity). Caveats: conifers, no outline data, and the paper itself calls the evidence for increased resistance in older trees "not conclusive". The earlier premise that laterals escape the limit is withdrawn; the step to a *dome* rather than a flat cap is still our inference.
2. **Leader dieback and replacement** (1.7). Up from sixth: Koch 2004 observed multiple tops from repeated leader death in very tall redwoods, linked to the same hydraulic limit, which connects mechanism 1 to the takeover step in mechanism 3. Broadleaf timing is unmeasured.
3. **Loss of apical control as the leader slows, plus sympodial takeover** (1.1, 1.3). Down from first. Wilson 2000 [full text, user copy] supports that apical control is weaker in slow, old and well-lit branches (by citation) and that release lets a branch take over, but a takeover *restores* the leader and branching pattern; the experiments are mostly conifers; the age link is cited, not measured. Innes 1998 [full text, user copy] cuts against it for beech: the rounded phase is the vigorous one, and the slowing-leader phases are spiky and fragmented with more acute branch angles. Sequential reiteration after a differentiation threshold (Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007 [PMC]; ADR 0031 points here as R2 reiteration) is unchanged. The evidence is mechanistic and architectural, not a measured rounding onset.
4. **Reiteration and re-crowning** (1.6). Strong as a crown-builder, confirmed in full text for sequential reiteration [PMC]; the beech re-crowning papers are about suppressed trees, so weaker for the open-grown dome. Unchanged.
5. **Branch arching and angle release** (1.5). Plausible; the mechanics (self-load against growth-stress moment) and release-turns-up are both now sourced to Wilson 2000 (a review, mainly conifers); no broadleaf measurement.
6. **Flank light** (1.4). Theory-level (Horn, Kuuluvainen); no direct test found. Likely shapes the envelope rather than triggering rounding.

Consistency with ADR 0031: the model has apical-control loss through Borchert-Honda λ and an age switch, and the shed rules; it lacks mechanism 1's path-length limit on extension and mechanism 3's takeover. ADR 0031's own result (no shedding rounds the lone crown) says the interior is not the problem; this ranking agrees the top-shell dynamics are.

### 5.2 Cheap diagnostics (targets are the model's own, since no measured targets were retrievable)
- **Crown radius and W/H against DBH for a lone tree**, not against age (Pretzsch 2015 has no time axis). Compare the model's open-grown crown radius at d 25 and 60 cm with the 95%-quantile lines in section 3: it should sit near or below the line, since the line is the open-grown upper edge. The sign of α_cr − α_h is a cheap species check: oak (and lime) should widen relative to height as girth grows; beech, hornbeam, sycamore, rowan, pine and fir should not. W/H rising with size is therefore a rounding signature for oak only; for beech and hornbeam use outline fullness and top-fifth leaf share instead. The lime plot means in section 2 (0.33 → 0.45) agree in direction with Pretzsch's lime exponent. Hemery 2005's woodland lines (section 3) give a lower, stand-grown reference: at d = 25 cm they are 0.54 (beech) to 0.94 (ash) of the Pretzsch open-grown crown diameter, 0.86 for oak. A lone model tree should sit at or below the Pretzsch line at both sizes; one above it is too wide.
- **Leader versus lateral extension rate** by age: mean annual leader extension against mean extension of the top-third laterals. A dome needs the ratio to fall toward 1 or below. This is the quantity the Roloff/Innes scale measures in practice (upper-crown shoot length), and the beech anchors are in 1.3: leaders above 30 cm per year in the vigorous phase, about 20 cm per year at the first change (Woodcock et al. 1995), a few cm per year in stagnation. Caution from Innes: in real beech this fall in leader growth goes with spiky, brush-like twigs and more acute branch angles, so a falling ratio is **not by itself** a dome signal; read it with the outline fullness and branch-angle checks.
- **Extension against root path length** (new, from Ryan & Yoder): record, for the shoots of a mature lone crown, annual extension against path length from the root. A path-length limit predicts extension falling at the top and at the tips of long branches together; if only the leader falls, the model does not have the mechanism the paper describes.
- **Branch angle of the upper crown** against leader growth: the finding of acute angles below about 20 cm per year (Woodcock et al. 1995, via Innes) is a testable sign.
- **Top-fifth leaf share** for the lone crown: ADR 0031 measured 22-24% with no shedding (rounded) against 38-46% with it (cap). Use as a regression check for any fix.
- **Outline fullness**: an ellipse is 0.79 (species-traits). A dome should sit between a cone of the same W/H and the ellipse.
- **Heading of top-shell axes**: fraction more than a threshold off vertical, rising with age. Needs no external data.

### 5.3 Revised stance
The ranking changed in the fourth pass. The hydraulic limit (now mechanism 1) moved up and leader dieback (now 2) moved up from last; loss of apical control and takeover (now 3) moved down, because Wilson's full text shows takeover restoring the leader pattern and Innes's shows the rounded beech phase as the vigorous one. This is a reading of the sources, not a measurement: no paper in the set measures a rounding onset or a W/H trajectory for a broadleaf. The cheapest model test is still a limit on extension, but its form changes: it must be a cap on **path length from the root for every shoot** (the paper's mechanism), not a leader-only cap with free laterals. Judge it against the model's own targets in 5.2 and against the paper's own predicted outcome (flattened top, thickened limbs), which may be the flat-cap defect rather than a dome; the top-fifth leaf share and outline fullness checks tell the two apart.
