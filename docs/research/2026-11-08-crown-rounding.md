# How do real crowns become rounded? (issue #74)

_Researched 2026-11-08 for the wayfinder map (#73). Question: what makes a crown a dome with age, which species do it, and what could a cheap diagnostic check?_

**What this note does not repeat.** Reiteration as a crown-builder (Millet et al. 1999; Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007) is in [species-identity](2026-11-01-species-identity.md) (Reiteration section); the simulators' levers (phase change at maturity, sympodial takeover, λ) are in [simulators](2026-11-01-simulators.md); the flat-cap diagnostics are ADR 0031; species outline descriptions (hornbeam cone to dome, beech wide dome, birch ovoid) are in species-identity and species-traits. This note adds the mechanisms and their sources.

**Verification tags.** [abs] = abstract or catalogue record read this session; [sec] = reported by a search summary of the source, source itself not read; [mem] = from memory, not checked this session; treat as a lead. Publisher sites (Springer, Elsevier, Wiley) refused fetching, so **no full text was read for any source below**. **No quantitative per-species rounding onset (age, height, W/H) was retrievable.** Where numbers are wanted, section 5.3 says which paper to open.

## 1. Mechanisms, with sources

### 1.1 Loss of apical control with age and size
- Wilson (2000), *Am. J. Bot.* 87(5): 601-607, "Apical control of branch growth and angle in woody plants" [abs]: apical control is inhibition of a lateral by the shoots above it; it begins once lateral buds break and leaf; it **also limits older, large branches**; under it, more of the branch's assimilate goes to the stem, so the branch is starved even with adequate hormone. Removing the upper shoots lets a branch grow and often turn upward. Hormonal mechanism unresolved.
- Reading (ours): a leader that slows (1.2) or dies releases the laterals beneath it; the strongest takes over as a new leader (sympodial takeover, already row 5 of the simulators note's gap table). This is the generative step of a dome. The broadleaf sources for it are the Troll-model and reiteration papers already reviewed.
- Architectural-model classification: beech follows Troll's model and maple Rauh's [sec, from a search summary of Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007]. Troll's model is plagiotropic with sympodial axes, so a flat or domed outline is built in; Rauh's is orthotropic with monopodial axes and gives a more conical young tree [mem]. Hallé, Oldeman & Tomlinson (1978), *Tropical Trees and Forests*, Springer, and Oldeman (1974), *L'architecture de la forêt guyanaise*, Mémoires ORSTOM 73, introduced the models and reiteration [abs, bibliographic only].

### 1.2 Hydraulic and height limits
- Ryan & Yoder (1997), *BioScience* 47(4): 235-242 [abs]: leaf-specific hydraulic conductance and stomatal conductance fall as trees grow taller, cutting assimilation; the paper favours hydraulic limitation as the most promising explanation for growth decline with age. Ryan, Phillips & Bond (2006) revisit: hydraulic limitation of gas exchange with size is common, not universal [sec].
- Consequence for shape (our inference, not claimed by these papers): when leader extension is capped by path-length resistance but lateral shoots, which have short paths, keep their supply, the crown widens relative to its height. W/H rises with age at roughly constant H. Sterck & Schieving (2007), *Ecol. Monogr.* 77(3): 405-420 [abs], a 3-D functional-structural model, report expansion and steady-state phases, height limits and allometric scaling emerging from selection for net carbon gain, with 3-D structure changing through ontogeny. They are the closest published model analogue to this project; the full text should say whether crowns round in the steady-state phase.
- Koch et al. (2004), *Nature* 428: 851-854, redwood height limit set by leaf water potential [mem].

### 1.3 Crown development phases (empirical sequence)
- Roloff's four phases for deciduous trees: exploration, degeneration, stagnation, resignation [sec; Roloff's originals not read]. Reading (ours): exploration builds a young crown with strong leaders; degeneration and stagnation mark the main leader losing dominance and the outer shell becoming a rounded envelope of shorter shoots; resignation is the old, retrenching crown. Roloff also uses shoot-base scars to reconstruct 10+ years of shoot elongation [sec]. This is the one source found that offers a **staged, measurable** rounding sequence (shoot length by crown position over time).

### 1.4 Light geometry: side light and flank loading
- Horn (1971), *The Adaptive Geometry of Trees*, Princeton: multilayer crowns suit full sun, monolayer crowns suit shade [sec]. Kuuluvainen (1992), *Oikos* 65: 275-284 [abs, partial]: sun elevation by latitude favours narrow crowns at high latitude and wider, flatter or domed crowns at low latitude; a later modelling study finds the latitude effect weaker than claimed, working mainly through site productivity [sec].
- Reading (ours): for a lone tree, light is highest at the top and flanks, so the outer shell is the productive surface; an envelope that maximises exposed shell for a given foliage volume is round. In stands the flank is shaded and the same rule gives a tall narrow crown (the stand's lid, ADR 0031). No source found tests flank light as the specific cause of rounding.

### 1.5 Branch reorientation and arching
- Wilson (2000) above [abs]: branch angle is itself under apical control; released branches turn up. Arching of long limbs under their own load is standard mechanics [mem]; no primary measurement retrieved. Together these give the "ascending co-dominant limbs that arch at the tips" ADR 0031 names. Not yet sourced to a primary measurement.

### 1.6 Reiteration and re-crowning
- Nicolini, Chanson & Bonne (2001), *Ann. Bot.* 87(6): 737-750 [abs, partial]: suppressed understorey beech are re-crowned by epicormic branches as the canopy closes; stem cambial activity drops sharply with the new epicormic crown and then girth growth resumes. This is a **suppressed-tree** route, not the open-grown one.
- Nicolini (1997) and Nicolini & Chanson (1999) give stage-by-stage architecture of *Fagus sylvatica* (cited in Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007, Fig. 27) [sec]; originals not found.

### 1.7 Leader dieback
- Oak and hornbeam outlines in species-identity already carry "stag-headed" and "domed" wording (TSO, JRC). No primary measurement of dieback timing was found. Treat leader dieback as a descriptive, probably real, route to 1.1.

## 2. Which species round, which stay conical

Evidence found is **descriptive**, mostly already in species-identity. New here: the architecture-model split in 1.1 (Troll for beech, Rauh for maple [sec]) and Millet et al. 1999 (already cited elsewhere): birch and aspen have almost no total reiteration, so stay excurrent with a narrow aperture; maple and beech use total reiteration, which "promotes lateral spread". Conifer pyramids are kept by strong apical control until old age; for fir and spruce the existing docs record cone to dome with age.

Not found: onset age or height for oak, maple, beech, hornbeam, lime. No lime-specific source was retrieved at all.

## 3. Open-grown versus stand-grown
- Pretzsch et al. (2015), *Urban For. Urban Green.* 14: 466-479, DOI 10.1016/j.ufug.2015.04.006 [abs]: 22 species clustered into 5 crown-extension types by open-grown crown-radius to stem-diameter allometry, with mean height, crown radius, projection area and volume against diameter per type. Species assignment and coefficients are in the paper, not the abstract. **The most useful single source to open next**: it would calibrate a W/H diagnostic directly.
- Hemery, Savill & Pryor (2005), *For. Ecol. Manage.* 215: 285-294, DOI 10.1016/j.foreco.2005.05.016 [abs]: crown diameter against DBH is a straight line for open-grown British broadleaves; prediction intervals are at least 20% of the value for some species. Earlier work covered only oak (Jobling & Pearce 1977). Coefficients (K = crown diameter / DBH) are in the paper.
- Pretzsch (2014), *For. Ecol. Manage.* 327: 251-264 [abs]: mixed canopies fill space more densely and plastically; crown-projection sums are higher in mixtures. Bears on the stand case, not on rounding itself.
- Reading (ours): open-grown crowns are limited by the tree's own supply, so the hydraulic and apical-control routes dominate; stand-grown crowns are limited by neighbours' light and stay narrow, with recession from below (ADR 0031's lid). Beech's long clean bole in stands and low massive limbs open-grown is in species-identity.

## 4. What is not known from this search
- The age or height at which any named species rounds, in any units.
- A published W/H trajectory (age on x) for any species.
- A direct test of side light or arching as a cause.
- Whether Roloff's phases correspond to a particular outline change.

## 5. Implications for the model

### 5.1 Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence for rounding the open-grown broadleaf crown
1. **Loss of apical control as the leader slows, plus sympodial takeover** (1.1, 1.3). Best supported conceptually (Wilson 2000 [abs]; Troll model for beech [sec]; ADR 0031 already points here as R2 reiteration). The evidence is mechanistic and architectural, not a measured rounding onset.
2. **Hydraulic or height limit on leader extension with lateral growth continuing** (1.2). Strong evidence for the limit (Ryan & Yoder [abs]); the step from limit to dome is our inference. Cheap to test in the model: if leader extension saturates with path length while laterals do not, does W/H rise?
3. **Reiteration and re-crowning** (1.6). Strong as a crown-builder (already reviewed); the beech re-crowning papers are about suppressed trees, so weaker for the open-grown dome.
4. **Branch arching and angle release** (1.5). Plausible; only the release-turns-up part is sourced (Wilson 2000).
5. **Flank light** (1.4). Theory-level (Horn, Kuuluvainen); no direct test found. Likely shapes the envelope rather than triggering rounding.
6. **Leader dieback** (1.7). Descriptive only.

Consistency with ADR 0031: the model has apical-control loss through Borchert-Honda λ and an age switch, and the shed rules; it lacks mechanism 2's separate leader cap and mechanism 1's takeover. ADR 0031's own result (no shedding rounds the lone crown) says the interior is not the problem; this ranking agrees the top-shell dynamics are.

### 5.2 Cheap diagnostics (targets are the model's own, since no measured targets were retrievable)
- **W/H against age for a lone tree**: should rise through life and plateau, not stay constant or fall. External anchors to fetch: Pretzsch 2015 type allometries and Hemery 2005 K. Compare the model's crown diameter to the K x DBH line; it should fall inside the paper's prediction band (about 20% or wider).
- **Leader versus lateral extension rate** by age: mean annual leader extension against mean extension of the top-third laterals. A dome needs the ratio to fall toward 1 or below.
- **Top-fifth leaf share** for the lone crown: ADR 0031 measured 22-24% with no shedding (rounded) against 38-46% with it (cap). Use as a regression check for any fix.
- **Outline fullness**: an ellipse is 0.79 (species-traits). A dome should sit between a cone of the same W/H and the ellipse.
- **Heading of top-shell axes**: fraction more than a threshold off vertical, rising with age. Needs no external data.
