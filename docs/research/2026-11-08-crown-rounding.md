# How do real crowns become rounded? (issue #74)

_Researched 2026-11-08 for the wayfinder map (#73). Question: what makes a crown a dome with age, which species do it, and what could a cheap diagnostic check?_

**What this note does not repeat.** Reiteration as a crown-builder (Millet et al. 1999; Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007) is in [species-identity](2026-11-01-species-identity.md) (Reiteration section); the simulators' levers (phase change at maturity, sympodial takeover, λ) are in [simulators](2026-11-01-simulators.md); the flat-cap diagnostics are ADR 0031; species outline descriptions (hornbeam cone to dome, beech wide dome, birch ovoid) are in species-identity and species-traits. This note adds the mechanisms and their sources.

**Second pass (2026-11-08, full-text access).** The first pass read abstracts only. This pass added a headless-browser fetcher, PMC, OpenAlex open-access locations and repository copies. Each claim is re-checked against the best text obtained, and the tags say where that text came from. Five sources were read in full or searched in full; the rest stay abstract-only because no legal copy could be fetched (list at the end). No shadow libraries were used.

**Verification tags.** [PMC] = PMC full text; [OA] = open-access publisher copy; [arXiv] = arXiv/HAL preprint full text; [abs] = abstract or repository record only; [sec] = reported by a search summary, source not read; [mem] = from memory, a lead only. **Still no measured per-species rounding onset (age, height, W/H) in hand**: the paper that would give it (Pretzsch et al. 2015) was located in two legal copies but both were behind bot walls (see the last section).

## 1. Mechanisms, with sources

### 1.1 Loss of apical control with age and size
- Wilson (2000), *Am. J. Bot.* 87(5): 601-607, DOI 10.2307/2656846, "Apical control of branch growth and angle in woody plants" [abs; JSTOR, closed]: apical control is inhibition of a lateral by the shoots above it; it begins once lateral buds break and leaf; it **also limits older, large branches**; under it, more of the branch's assimilate goes to the stem, so the branch is starved even with adequate hormone. Removing the upper shoots lets a branch grow and often turn upward. Hormonal mechanism unresolved.
- Reading (ours): a leader that slows (1.2) or dies releases the laterals beneath it; the strongest takes over as a new leader (sympodial takeover, already row 5 of the simulators note's gap table). This is the generative step of a dome. The broadleaf sources for it are the Troll-model and reiteration papers already reviewed.
- Architectural-model classification: **beech Troll, maple Rauh is not supported by the full text** of Barthélémy & Caraglio (2007), *Ann. Bot.* 99: 375-407 [PMC2802949, read]. What it does say: Rauh's model has rhythmic growth, all axes monopodial, lateral sexuality; Acer sp. is drawn as a monopodial system and Ulmus sp. and Platanus as sympodial (after Troll 1937); sympodial successions can mimic a monopodial axis ("pseudomonopodium"), so an outline cannot be read back to a model. The beech-Troll and maple-Rauh labels stay [mem]. The same paper says gradual architectural change with development stage in *Fagus sylvatica* was described by Nicolini (1997) and Nicolini & Chanson (1999) (its Fig. 27), which are the stage sequences to find. Hallé, Oldeman & Tomlinson (1978) and Oldeman (1974) introduced the models and reiteration [abs, bibliographic only].

### 1.2 Hydraulic and height limits
- Ryan & Yoder (1997), *BioScience* 47(4): 235-242, DOI 10.2307/1313077 [abs; closed]: leaf-specific hydraulic conductance and stomatal conductance fall as trees grow taller, cutting assimilation; the paper favours hydraulic limitation as the most promising explanation for growth decline with age. Ryan, Phillips & Bond (2006) revisit: hydraulic limitation of gas exchange with size is common, not universal [sec].
- Consequence for shape (our inference, not claimed by these papers): when leader extension is capped by path-length resistance but lateral shoots, which have short paths, keep their supply, the crown widens relative to its height. W/H rises with age at roughly constant H. Sterck & Schieving (2007), "3-D growth patterns of trees", *Ecol. Monogr.* 77(3): 405-420, DOI 10.1890/06-1670.1 [abs; Wageningen repository record, full text not obtained], a 3-D functional-structural model, report a sigmoid expansion phase and a stable steady-state phase, limits to tree size including height, and constant allometric scaling in the expansion phase, all emerging from selection for net carbon gain. Their assumptions include new meristems placed where branches fall (so a viable meristem population stays inside the crown) and metamers placed where carbon benefit over a leaf pipe's life is greatest. The abstract does not describe the crown outline. They are the closest published model analogue to this project; the full text should say whether crowns round in the steady-state phase.
- Koch et al. (2004), *Nature* 428: 851-854, redwood height limit set by leaf water potential [mem; a Zenodo copy exists but its files are restricted].

### 1.3 Crown development phases (empirical sequence)
- Roloff's four phases are exploration, degeneration, stagnation and resignation. Innes (1998), *Forestry* 71(2): 113-130, DOI 10.1093/forestry/71.2.113 [abs, repository record; a WSL copy exists but only the abstract rendered] reviews them for **beech (*Fagus sylvatica*)**: four stages "generally recognized"; the classes "probably reflect the progressive deterioration of the crown"; scoring is affected by many factors, as defoliation scores are; **measuring shoot elongation in the upper crown is a more useful measure** but needs destructive sampling and is slow. A search summary gives beech apical shoot growth of about 5 cm/yr in the worst decline class against 43 cm/yr in healthy trees (Roloff 1989, *Trees*) [sec]. So the scale is a vitality scale built on upper-crown shoot length. Roloff's original monographs (1985, 1988) are not open.
- Reading (ours, weaker than the first pass): upper-crown shoot length falling over a tree's life is the measurable quantity behind the phases, and it is the same signal as the leader/lateral extension ratio proposed in 5.2. The phases do not by themselves document a change of outline.

### 1.4 Light geometry: side light and flank loading
- Horn (1971), *The Adaptive Geometry of Trees*, Princeton: multilayer crowns suit full sun, monolayer crowns suit shade [sec]. Kuuluvainen (1992), *Oikos* 65: 275-284 [abs, partial]: sun elevation by latitude favours narrow crowns at high latitude and wider, flatter or domed crowns at low latitude; a later modelling study finds the latitude effect weaker than claimed, working mainly through site productivity [sec].
- Reading (ours): for a lone tree, light is highest at the top and flanks, so the outer shell is the productive surface; an envelope that maximises exposed shell for a given foliage volume is round. In stands the flank is shaded and the same rule gives a tall narrow crown (the stand's lid, ADR 0031). No source found tests flank light as the specific cause of rounding.

### 1.5 Branch reorientation and arching
- Wilson (2000) above [abs]: branch angle is itself under apical control; released branches turn up. Arching of long limbs under their own load is standard mechanics [mem]; no primary measurement retrieved. Together these give the "ascending co-dominant limbs that arch at the tips" ADR 0031 names. Not yet sourced to a primary measurement.

### 1.6 Reiteration and re-crowning
- Nicolini, Chanson & Bonne (2001), *Ann. Bot.* 87(6): 737-750, DOI 10.1006/anbo.2001.1398 [abs; closed]: suppressed understorey beech are re-crowned by epicormic branches as the canopy closes; stem cambial activity drops sharply with the new epicormic crown and then girth growth resumes. This is a **suppressed-tree** route, not the open-grown one. A GreenLab beech paper [arXiv, Letort et al. 2008, searched in full] cites it for epicormics that replace a deficient crown, observed on one of its two understorey trees but not modelled. Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007 [PMC] separate **adaptive reiteration** (a response to increased resources) from **traumatic** reiteration (after loss of structure) and **sequential** (automatic) reiteration, which occurs after a definite threshold of differentiation and is "a common feature of tree development and crown construction". Sequential reiteration is thus an age-programmed route to a multi-leader crown, the nearest sourced analogue of a rounding trigger.
- Nicolini (1997) and Nicolini & Chanson (1999) give stage-by-stage architecture of *Fagus sylvatica* (cited in Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007, Fig. 27 [PMC]); originals not found open. Colin et al. 2012, *Ann. Bot.* 110: 995-1005 [PMC, read] is on beech trunk epicormics (they sprout less where radial growth is high) and has nothing on outline.

### 1.7 Leader dieback
- Oak and hornbeam outlines in species-identity already carry "stag-headed" and "domed" wording (TSO, JRC). No primary measurement of dieback timing was found. Treat leader dieback as a descriptive, probably real, route to 1.1.

## 2. Which species round, which stay conical

Evidence found is **descriptive**, mostly already in species-identity. New here: the architecture-model split in 1.1 (Troll for beech, Rauh for maple [sec]) and Millet et al. 1999 (already cited elsewhere): birch and aspen have almost no total reiteration, so stay excurrent with a narrow aperture; maple and beech use total reiteration, which "promotes lateral spread". Conifer pyramids are kept by strong apical control until old age; for fir and spruce the existing docs record cone to dome with age.

Not found: onset age or height for oak, maple, beech, hornbeam. **Lime, one weak anchor:** Martynova (2020), *SEEFOR* 11(1): 51-59, DOI 10.15177/seefor.20-04 [OA, read], measured small-leaved lime (*Tilia cordata*) in Ufa, Russia, twice, 11 years apart. In a park stand (stand density 1.0; n = 626 then 623): height 18.2 to 20.8 m, DBH 18.4 to 19.7 cm, crown diameter 6.0 to 9.4 m, so W/H rose from 0.33 to 0.45 while height rose 14%. Along a pruned street alley (n = 727 then 688): height 13.1 to 14.0 m, DBH 30.1 to 34.1 cm, crown diameter 9.0 to 10.2 m, W/H 0.69 to 0.73. The paper reports crown length falling from 8.5 to 3.67 m in the park, which sits oddly with the widening crown and is not explained in the text, so treat it as a definition or measurement quirk. The data are plot means, not individual trees followed through life, from managed urban plantings; they show crown width rising faster than height in mature lime and nothing about onset.

## 3. Open-grown versus stand-grown
- Pretzsch et al. (2015), *Urban For. Urban Green.* 14: 466-479, DOI 10.1016/j.ufug.2015.04.006 [OA, read in full from the user's copy]. 39,057 trees of 22 species, mostly south German long-term plots plus nine cities; ages about 20 to over 200 years; pruned solitary trees excluded where possible.
  - **Method:** quantile regression of ln(cr) = a + α·ln(d) (crown radius cr in m, d = DBH in cm). The **95% quantile stands for open-grown trees** (widest crown at a given d), 50% for average and 5% for suppressed. Height uses the **5% quantile** of ln(h) = a + α·ln(d) for open-grown trees (lowest height at a given d).
  - **Crown-extension types** (k-means on cr25 and α): type 1 "large crown, moderate slope" = *Tilia cordata*, *Carpinus betulus*, *Fagus sylvatica*, *Ulmus glabra*, *Aesculus*, *Platanus*, *Robinia*, *Khaya* (only broadleaves); type 2 "medium, medium slope" = *Betula pendula*, *Pinus sylvestris*, *Fraxinus excelsior*, *Larix*, *Alnus*; type 3 "medium, steep slope" = *Quercus robur*, *Picea glauca*, *Pseudotsuga*; type 4 "medium, flat slope" = *Acer pseudoplatanus*, *Sorbus aucuparia*, *Picea abies*, *Abies alba*. Types do not follow phylogeny.
  - **The crown-radius exponent** α_cr ranges from 0.39 to 0.83 and clusters near 2/3, but oak's (0.816) is among the steepest and beech's (0.471) among the flattest: an oak keeps widening its crown with girth, while a beech widens it early and then slows.
  - **Open-grown coefficients for the model's species** (Tables 2 and 4; cr at the 95% quantile, h at the 5%):

    | Species | a_cr | α_cr | cr25 (m) | a_h | α_h | h25 (m) | α_cr − α_h |

    | *Quercus robur* | −1.437 | 0.816 | 3.3 | −0.524 | 0.716 | 5.9 | +0.10 |

  - **Derived W/H = 2·cr/h** (my arithmetic from the table, not a figure in the paper): oak 1.11 at d 25 → 1.21 at d 60; lime 0.83 → 1.15; birch 0.52 → 0.57; sycamore 0.41 → 0.39; hornbeam 0.57 → 0.49; beech 0.72 → 0.51; rowan 0.39 → 0.30; pine 0.39 → 0.30; silver fir 0.32 → 0.25. **Caveats:** cr and h come from opposite quantiles, so this is an envelope (widest crown over shortest stem), not one tree's shape, and it overstates W/H. Oak's height fit rests on 69 trees and its h25 of 5.9 m looks low (urban Cape Town trees are in the sample). Sycamore and rowan heights are from forest plots. Read the trends, not the levels.
  - **No time axis.** The authors say the relationships give crown size at a given diameter, not when it is reached; diameter growth is too variable to build general age–diameter curves. So the paper calibrates shape against **girth**, not against age.
  - **What it means for rounding:** only oak and lime (and, weakly, birch) widen faster than they grow tall as girth increases (α_cr > α_h). Beech, hornbeam, sycamore, rowan and the conifers get *relatively narrower* with size when open-grown. If "rounding" means W/H rising with size, this dataset supports it for oak and lime only; beech and hornbeam keep broad crowns (type 1, the largest cr25) without W/H rising. A dome for beech and hornbeam would therefore be about **outline shape** (fullness of the top), not W/H.
- Hemery, Savill & Pryor (2005), *For. Ecol. Manage.* 215: 285-294, DOI 10.1016/j.foreco.2005.05.016 [abs; closed, no repository copy found]: crown diameter against DBH is a straight line for open-grown British broadleaves; prediction intervals are at least 20% of the value for some species. Earlier work covered only oak (Jobling & Pearce 1977). Coefficients (K = crown diameter / DBH) are in the paper.
- Pretzsch (2014), *For. Ecol. Manage.* 327: 251-264 [abs]: mixed canopies fill space more densely and plastically; crown-projection sums are higher in mixtures. Bears on the stand case, not on rounding itself.
- Reading (ours): open-grown crowns are limited by the tree's own supply, so the hydraulic and apical-control routes dominate; stand-grown crowns are limited by neighbours' light and stay narrow, with recession from below (ADR 0031's lid). Beech's long clean bole in stands and low massive limbs open-grown is in species-identity.

## 4. What is still not known
- The age or height at which any named species rounds, in any units.
- A published W/H trajectory (age on x) for any species.
- A direct test of side light or arching as a cause.
- Whether Roloff's phases correspond to a particular outline change (Innes 1998 describes them as a decline scale).
- Which architectural model beech and maple follow (the first pass's Troll/Rauh claim failed its check).

## 5. Implications for the model

### 5.1 Candidate mechanisms, ranked by evidence for rounding the open-grown broadleaf crown
1. **Loss of apical control as the leader slows, plus sympodial takeover** (1.1, 1.3). Best supported conceptually (Wilson 2000 [abs]; sequential reiteration after a differentiation threshold, Barthélémy & Caraglio 2007 [PMC]; ADR 0031 already points here as R2 reiteration). The beech-Troll label that backed this in the first pass is withdrawn. The evidence is mechanistic and architectural, not a measured rounding onset.
2. **Hydraulic or height limit on leader extension with lateral growth continuing** (1.2). Strong evidence for the limit (Ryan & Yoder [abs]); the step from limit to dome is our inference. Cheap to test in the model: if leader extension saturates with path length while laterals do not, does W/H rise?
3. **Reiteration and re-crowning** (1.6). Strong as a crown-builder, now confirmed in full text for sequential reiteration [PMC]; the beech re-crowning papers are about suppressed trees, so weaker for the open-grown dome.
4. **Branch arching and angle release** (1.5). Plausible; only the release-turns-up part is sourced (Wilson 2000).
5. **Flank light** (1.4). Theory-level (Horn, Kuuluvainen); no direct test found. Likely shapes the envelope rather than triggering rounding.
6. **Leader dieback** (1.7). Descriptive only.

Consistency with ADR 0031: the model has apical-control loss through Borchert-Honda λ and an age switch, and the shed rules; it lacks mechanism 2's separate leader cap and mechanism 1's takeover. ADR 0031's own result (no shedding rounds the lone crown) says the interior is not the problem; this ranking agrees the top-shell dynamics are.

### 5.2 Cheap diagnostics (targets are the model's own, since no measured targets were retrievable)
- **Crown radius and W/H against DBH for a lone tree**, not against age (Pretzsch 2015 has no time axis). Compare the model's open-grown crown radius at d 25 and 60 cm with the 95%-quantile lines in section 3: it should sit near or below the line, since the line is the open-grown upper edge. The sign of α_cr − α_h is a cheap species check: oak (and lime) should widen relative to height as girth grows; beech, hornbeam, sycamore, rowan, pine and fir should not. W/H rising with size is therefore a rounding signature for oak only; for beech and hornbeam use outline fullness and top-fifth leaf share instead. The lime plot means in section 2 (0.33 → 0.45) agree in direction with Pretzsch's lime exponent. Hemery 2005's K line remains unread.
- **Leader versus lateral extension rate** by age: mean annual leader extension against mean extension of the top-third laterals. A dome needs the ratio to fall toward 1 or below. This is the quantity the Roloff/Innes scale measures in practice (upper-crown shoot length), so real beech data of this kind exist; the open problem is getting them.
- **Top-fifth leaf share** for the lone crown: ADR 0031 measured 22-24% with no shedding (rounded) against 38-46% with it (cap). Use as a regression check for any fix.
- **Outline fullness**: an ellipse is 0.79 (species-traits). A dome should sit between a cone of the same W/H and the ellipse.
- **Heading of top-shell axes**: fraction more than a threshold off vertical, rising with age. Needs no external data.

### 5.3 Revised stance
The ranking in 5.1 stands but its evidence base is thinner than the first pass implied: mechanism 1 now rests on Wilson's abstract plus the generic reiteration literature, not on a verified beech-Troll classification. The cheapest model test remains mechanism 2 (a leader cap that lateral shoots do not share), judged against the model's own targets in 5.2.
