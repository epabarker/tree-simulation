## 1. What the model does today

- **Seeding is a size threshold, never an age** (`r1.maturity`, a height in cells; `src/core/params.ts`). A cell is 0.25 m (ADR 0049), so the default 70 cells is 17.5 m; the `forest` preset sets 40 (10 m); species override it: maple and holly 30 (7.5 m), rowan 20 (5 m), willow 25 (6.3 m); birch, oak, beech, fir, hornbeam and pine inherit the preset (`src/core/flora.ts`). Above it, a share `fRep` of surplus carbon (preset 0.1) goes to seed at `cSeed` carbon per seed (1.5 to 10 by species).
- **Recruits** land by a Laplace kernel (`d`, 12 to 60 cells), then establish if ground light is at least `qEst` (0.02 to 0.3) with probability `pEst` = 0.3, under a `maxTrees` cap. `m0` (0.002 to 0.012) is background mortality per tree per season.
- **Seed rain from outside** (ADR 0018) exists only for goat willow (1 seed per 100 cells per season) and birch (0.2). Seedlings that fail `qEst` are simply not created; there is no seedling bank that waits in shade and dies slowly.
- **The garden** plants one row and keeps recruits to a depth band (`o4.band`), with `maxTrees` per page (`src/web/plate.test.ts`: 24 to 75 by width, 229 with `recruits: 1`).

So the model has no age at first seed (only a height), no seedling class below an established tree, no mast variation, and recruits are born at the establishment size. Everything in section 4 is about those gaps.

## 2. Age at first seed, and interval between seed years

Primary source: the USDA _Woody Plant Seed Manual_ (Bonner & Karrfalt 2008, Agric. Handbook 727), genus chapters, which I read from the full volume ([Treesearch 32626](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/32626)). It reports _minimum_ seed-bearing age and the years between "large seedcrops". Minimum age is the earliest scattered seed, not the age at which a stand's trees seed reliably.

| Species | Minimum seed-bearing age (yr) | Years between large crops | Mature height (m) | Source and caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silver birch _Betula pendula_ | 15 (WPSM); flowering from 5–10 (Atkinson) | 2–3 (WPSM); Atkinson gives no interval, only that crops vary from year to year | 19.5 | WPSM _Betula_ Table 3 (after Brinkman 1974). Downy birch _B. pubescens_ is the same. Atkinson 1992 _[full text, user copy]_: flowering "usually starts" at 5–10 years (Johnsson 1949), and exceptional trees flower in their second year. So WPSM's 15 is later than the flora's flowering age by a factor of 1.5–3; the flora gives no age at which a stand's birches seed reliably, and flowering is not a viable crop (empty fruits form without pollination; in the flora's 1991 samples the filled share ranged from under 1% to about 21%). Life span: usually under 100 years, one tree at least 116, maturing at 50–60 years in the south and then dying back rapidly, but healthy at 180 in central Scotland (Gimingham 1984; Rackham 1980; Mitchell 1974, as cited). |

| Scots pine _Pinus sylvestris_ | 5–15 | 4–6 | 24–40 | WPSM _Pinus_ Table 2. The age is for cultivated and orchard trees; stand trees are later. |

Notes:

- **The seed manual's "mature height" is the species' height at maturity, not the height at first seed**, so it does not calibrate `r1.maturity`. I found no primary source that gives the height at first seed for these species.
- **Open-grown versus stand.** Only beech carries the split (40–50 v 60–80). It is the general rule: shaded crowns defer seed. The garden's trees are crowded, so the stand column, not the minimum, is the one to compare with.
- **Masting.** WPSM gives interval ranges (above). Packham et al. 2012 _[publisher HTML via browser, full text]_ add, for beech: flowering starts at about 40 years in the open and 60-80 in dense stands (Firbas 1949, as cited; agrees with WPSM); the masting pattern rests on an intrinsic biennial production that tends to cycles of 4-8 years in mainland Europe, with gaps of up to 15 years between mast years; a mast year can reach **1,500-4,000 seeds per m²** (Harmer 1994) or 307-1,168 per m² in Danish woods, of which 10-18% were fungus-infected and 10-12% empty (Olesen & Madsen 2008); the species has no persistent seed bank, since seeds germinate the spring after dispersal; and in the 1984 mast year at Wytham Woods 40% of the seed vanished within a month of falling and under 3% of full seed remained by March (Linnard 1987).
- **How low are ordinary years? The sourced figure.** Watt (1923), as cited by Packham et al.: the average annual seed production of ash is 33% of a full crop, and of beech and oak only **16-17%**, "so show greater variation between poor and mast years". That is the long-run mean across years as a share of a full crop, not the yield of a typical non-mast year. The first pass's guess that ordinary years give "a few percent" of a mast year is therefore removed. The nearest sourced numbers are that mean (16-17% of a full crop for beech and oak) and the beech seedling counts in section 3 (intermediate crops roughly 4 to 80 times smaller than the mast). I found no source for the median non-mast year. Atkinson 1992 _[full text, user copy]_ gives a birch figure: in Sarvas's (1948) experiments on _B. pendula_ the largest annual crop was 53,200 filled seeds per m², "a more normal maximum" 2,300, and the crop in the average year 340. So the average year is about 15% of the normal maximum and under 1% of the largest, of the same order as Watt's 16-17% of a full crop for beech and oak. These are one experimental series, not a stand survey, and the flora does not say how often the extremes occur. Seed rain of 3,800 to 43,000 seeds per m² was recorded over six years at Inverpolly in the Scottish Highlands (Miles & Kinnaird 1979a, as cited); the flora does not say whether this counts only filled seeds. The flora gives no interval between good years: it says only that birches produce varying quantities of seed each year.
- **Predator satiation** (Packham et al., citing Nilsson & Wastljung 1987): vertebrate seed destruction was lower in mast (5.7%) than non-mast (12%) years in the study cited, which is why masting works as a recruitment strategy.
- **Birch seed bank and dispersal** (Atkinson 1992, _[full text, user copy]_). The bank is short-lived. In two northern Swedish forest sites only 6% of sown seed was still viable after a year and the rate of decline slowed in the next two (Granstrom & Fries 1985); in British upland plantation soil birch seed showed no long-term survival and 80% of it was in the litter layer (Hill & Stevens 1981). Stored dry at 6 °C in plastic bags, germinability was halved after 8 years, but that is storage, not field soil. Gall midges destroy 0-10% of the crop and are judged unlikely to limit regeneration. **Most seeds fall within 40-50 m of the source** (Sarvas 1948), which is 160-200 cells at 0.25 m; the model's Laplace kernel `d` is 12-60 cells (section 1), and I have not checked how `d` maps to a typical distance, so the two are not compared here. Birch is unusual among fire-following species in regenerating from dispersed rather than soil-stored seed on Scottish heaths (Hobbs et al. 1984, as cited). So birch has a transient bank of about a year, which fits a model with no long seed bank.
- **Dropped.** The first pass's search-snippet figures for oak masting (a UK 18%-of-trees-give-51%-of-acorns study; a 27-year southern English record) and for birch seed counts (Huth 2009 as cited) could not be traced to a full text and are removed, not carried forward.

## 3. Seedlings and saplings: densities and loss rates

### Beech under a mature canopy (Czech Republic)

Peña, Remeš & Bílek 2010, _J For Sci_ 56(12): 580–588 (DOI 10.17221/69/2010-JFS), read in full; permanent plots in beech shelterwood (a managed stand with a harvest cut, not a closed old wood):

- **Mast year v ordinary year** _[publisher OA PDF, full text]_. The full mast of 2003 gave 78.1 to 298.8 thousand seedlings per hectare on the four plots. The intermediate crops of 2006, 2007 and 2008 gave 9.0–75.4, 0–5.2 and 3.8–16.9 thousand per hectare. Comparing plot ranges, the mast is roughly 4 to 30 times the 2006 crop and 5 to 80 times the 2008 crop, and 2007 was near zero. The first pass's "10 to 50 times" was a loose summary of these overlapping ranges; read it as an order of magnitude.
- **Where the loss falls.** Cohort 2003 lost most of its seedlings in its second year (2005): 63% and 39% on the two plots with border cutting. From 2007 mortality was 1–15% a year, "very constant", with 0–7% a year on the plots that were not cut. The authors conclude that after the second year the rate is low and independent of the initial number.
- **Fourth-year loss across density groups:** 17%, then 3% and 6% in the next two years.
- **Old stock:** in 2009 the plots held 58.1–247.8 thousand per hectare of seedlings older than the previous year, against 3.8–16.9 thousand in their first year; one plot (a denser stand with thick humus) had only 2.1 thousand.

- **Shade limit, beech seedlings** (Packham et al. 2012, citing Grubb et al. 1996: 110-day pots, 10 seedlings per treatment) _[publisher HTML via browser]_: all 10 beech seedlings died at 0.3% of full daylight; 1 of 10 and 3 of 10 died at 1.6% on the two soils; 0 or 1 of 10 at 11% and 63%. Beech seedlings have a compensation point of about 2% of full sun (Helliwell 2012, as cited).
- **Gaps for beech** (Packham et al., citing Peltier et al. 1997 and Watt 1923): germination and establishment of beech and ash were best in semi-shade in an old-growth beech forest; low recruitment in canopy gaps was attributed to poor soil conditions and to the dense ungrazed shrub and herb growth that follows gap formation, which excludes beech seedlings. So for beech, gaps are not simply better. This is a statement in the review, not a measured gap-v-canopy density pair.

### Birch seedlings: establishment and shade exclusion (Britain)

Atkinson 1992 _[full text, user copy]_ is a review, so every figure below is as it cites its sources.

- **First-year loss:** seedlings suffer 80-90% mortality in their first year, perhaps mainly from drought, with frost heaving a major cause of death over winter (Miles & Kinnaird 1979a; Kinnaird 1974). On wet sites at low irradiance damping-off can cause high mortality. Of 833 seeds germinating in a wet peaty soil at Inverpolly, only the five that formed mycorrhiza survived the first year.
- **Where birch establishes:** best on bare ground, particularly after burning; very poorly in even the lowest vegetation; seeds germinate on Sphagnum but rarely establish. The seed's reserves let a seedling reach only about 2 cm, against 8 cm for Scots pine, so a birch seedling cannot grow up through other vegetation.
- **Shade and birch stands:** the flora lists "intolerance of shade" among birch's main traits; very few seedlings survive closure of a heather canopy on moorland (Khoon & Gimingham 1984); _B. pendula_ leaves almost stop growing in shade but grow faster when lit again. Even-aged birch woods shade out any regeneration; birch saplings are rarely found within 15 m of older birches, and birch rarely regenerates in birch wood even when the canopy is open, which Burnett attributes partly to root competition. Two-aged stands occur, with saplings away from the immediate vicinity of mature trees.
- **Grazing and browsing:** sheep and deer grazing is often the main cause of regeneration failure; in one fenced Pennine woodland a sward that grew up after grazers were excluded prevented any new sapling birch recruiting over 26 years (Pigott 1983, as cited). The garden has no browsers (see the Sunart paragraph below).
- **No survival curve in shade.** The flora gives no time to death for seedlings under a canopy, only the statements above. The only quantitative shade-survival figures for birch I have are in Walters & Reich below, for American birches in pots.

### Time to death in deep shade (Ukraine, Uholka)

Petrovska et al. 2022 (_Eur J For Res_ 141: 43-58, DOI 10.1007/s10342-021-01427-3) _[publisher OA PDF, full text]_: 289 living and 179 dead juveniles (0-5 m tall) of beech, sycamore and Norway maple in six plots at **2-3% of diffuse light**. Model-predicted mean survival time, for slow growth (20-100 micrometres ring width) and the 131-200 cm height class, is **72 years for beech against 47-48 years for the two maples**. Age-specific mortality over 3 years was 0.03 for beech and 0.07-0.08 for the maples at LAR 10 cm² per g. Annual juvenile mortality elsewhere, as cited there: beech 3.3% and sycamore 7.3% (UK long-term plots, Kerr & Mackintosh 2012); beech 4.2-4.9% along a light gradient in NE France; 2% in a French sub-Mediterranean transect. Height extends survival more than leaf area ratio or growth. **These are shade-tolerant and mid-tolerant species; the paper has no light-demanders.**

Light-demander evidence. Walters & Reich 1996 (_Ecology_ 77: 841-853, DOI 10.2307/2265505) _[full text, user copy]_ grew seedlings of paper birch (_Betula papyrifera_, shade-intolerant), yellow birch (_B. alleghaniensis_, mid-tolerant), hop-hornbeam (_Ostrya virginiana_) and sugar maple (_Acer saccharum_) (both tolerant) and red oak (_Quercus rubra_, disturbance-adapted) for two growing seasons in outdoor shade houses at 2% and 8% of open-sky light (45 and 182 micromol per m² per s measured at the leaf), in 20-litre pots of forest soil, with and without added nitrogen. Nitrogen had no significant effect on survival or any trait, so the two nitrogen levels are pooled. These are potted, watered seedlings of North American congeners, started about 7 weeks after germination, in uniform light without the sun peaks of a forest floor (the authors' own caveat). Survival, read from the bars of Fig. 1 (to about 0.05) and the text:

| Light | Year | Paper birch | Yellow birch | Hop-hornbeam | Sugar maple | Red oak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2% | 1 | about 0.50 | about 0.47 | about 0.97 | about 1.0 | about 0.95 |
| 2% | 2 (of those alive) | 0 | 0 | about 0.22 | about 0.19 | about 0.11 |
| 8% | 1 | about 0.92 | about 0.92 | about 0.95 | about 0.95 | about 0.95 |
| 8% | 2 (of those alive) | about 0.83 | about 0.95 | about 0.56 | about 0.49 | about 0.37 |

- **At 2% light**: about 50% of both birches died in the first year and, of those alive that autumn, **all died in the second year**, against about 20% of hop-hornbeam and maple and 10% of red oak surviving. Two-year survival is therefore 0 for both birches, about 0.2 for the two tolerants and about 0.10 for oak (my products of the yearly figures, not numbers the paper states). Of the second-year deaths at 2% light about half came over the winter and half in the growing season; at 8% most came in winter. Birch growth at 2% was near zero, and second-year survival tracked first-year proportional growth (Spearman 0.82 at 2% light, P = 0.09, n = 5 species; 0.97 over both light levels, n = 10, P < 0.01).
- **At 8% light the ranking reverses**: second-year survival was about 70-80% for the two birches against 35-50% for hop-hornbeam, maple and red oak, and the birches grew fastest. So the birches' disadvantage is specific to very low light; at 8% they outlive the tolerants, which fits light-demanders that need a gap.
- **Correction to the abstract-based summary in the previous pass.** It said survival at 2% was higher in the tolerant species "than in birch and oak". The full text shows red oak's _first-year_ survival at 2% (about 0.95) matching the tolerants'; oak differs in its very low growth and in a second-year survival about half the tolerants' (about 0.11 against about 0.2). Red oak is not a near-total-loss species at 2% light over two years, but it is not a good survivor either. "At 8% birch did best and red oak worst" is true of growth; for second-year survival at 8% the tolerants (35-50%) were worse than the birches and close to oak.
- **Size and first-year loss.** The two birches started at a total dry mass of about 0.01 g against about 1.7 g for oak (about three orders of magnitude, Appendix) and had the highest first-year loss at 2% light; the authors read this as small seedlings being at high risk when they cannot escape small size by growth.
- **Scaling to a bank.** To show how small survival differences shape a seedling bank, the authors compute that annual survival of 20% against 10% from 10,000 seedlings leaves 16 against 1 after four years. They note that closed forest understories often receive under 2% of full daylight, and cite Kobe et al. 1995 for much lower mortality in saplings even at about 1% of full light (11% for yellow birch against 5% for sugar maple over 5 years), which they put down to larger saplings being less vulnerable than seedlings, or to method. So the seedling losses here are an upper bound for taller juveniles.

Cleavitt et al. 2024 (_Ecosphere_, DOI 10.1002/ecs2.4949) _[publisher OA HTML, abstract and discussion read]_ followed 1,733 marked northern red oak seedlings for 13 years at a range edge in New Hampshire: median survival 3-4 years, most deaths over winter, longer survival where oak seedling density was lower. It is an order of magnitude for an oak seedling bank, but for a range-edge American species, not a closed-canopy British oak wood, and the mortality is not attributed to shade alone.

### A primeval beech forest (Ukraine)

Commarmot et al. 2013, _Inventory of the Largest Primeval Beech Forest in Europe_ (WSL/Swiss-Ukrainian team, 314 plots, Uholka-Shyrokyi Luh, 2010; _[report PDF, full text]_; [report](https://www.slf.ch/fileadmin/user_upload/WSL/Mitarbeitende/hobi/Inventory_Report_2013.pdf), Tables 6.3–6.4, Fig. 6.6, 6.9, read):

- **Living trees at least 6 cm DBH: 435 ± 12 per hectare**, basal area 37 ± 1 m² per hectare, growing stock 582 ± 14 m³; 97% of basal area is beech; deadwood is 162 m³ per hectare; 10 trees per hectare are at least 80 cm DBH; the largest is 150 cm.
- **Regeneration (below 6 cm DBH): 34,642 ± 3,859 per hectare, about 3 per m²**, 69% of them under 40 cm tall. So the standing bank under a closed primeval canopy is of order 10⁴ per hectare, and the 40–129 cm and 130 cm-plus classes are far smaller (Fig. 6.6; the bars are in that figure, I read only the total and the 69%).
- **Other species** are found more often in the regeneration than in the canopy: sycamore, silver fir, elm and hornbeam (together under 3% of basal area). Willows and poplars are very rare, only at forest edges and rivers.
- **Diameter distribution** (Fig. 6.9, 4 cm classes) is a reversed J. The axis runs 0–160 per hectare per class, which I could not resolve more finely from the extracted text.
- **Structure:** mostly three-layered, most gaps no larger than one crown (WSL summary, [waldwissen.net](https://waldwissen.net/en/technique-and-planning/forest-inventory/inventory-of-the-largest-primeval-beech-forest-in-europe)).

### A grazed oak and birch woodland (Scotland)

SNH Research Report 1182 (2020) _[report PDF, full text]_, Sunart SSSI, 84 random plots, 2018: **313 live trees at least 5 cm DBH per hectare**, seedlings 1,834–1,904 per hectare (interquartile range 351–2,772), small saplings about 260 and large saplings about 82 per hectare. Sessile oak seedlings 18 per hectare against 42 mature and 96 over-mature oaks per hectare (one small sapling, no large ones); 70% of oak seedlings browsed. Downy birch seedlings 348, small saplings 177, large saplings 58, mature 39 per hectare, with no birch seedlings in 34 of 84 plots. Rowan seedlings 1,291–1,341 per hectare but only 47 and 11 saplings. Basal area exceeded 34 m² per hectare in only five of 84 plots, so shade limited saplings over under 10% of the wood. **Use:** a ceiling on what a heavily browsed British wood looks like (no oak recruitment), not a target; the garden has no browsers. The seedling and sapling size classes are the report's own.

### Oak: where recruitment is limited

Crawley & Long 1995, "Alternate bearing, predator satiation and seedling recruitment in _Quercus robur_ L." (_J Ecol_ 83: 683, DOI 10.2307/2261636) _[abstract only; no legal full text found: JSTOR only, and OpenAlex lists no open copy]_: oak recruitment was seed-limited in open sites with few herbivores and microsite-limited beneath a closed canopy; acorn losses to invertebrates followed predator satiation (a small crop loses a larger share). I have no density numbers from this paper. WPSM gives 30-50% acorn loss a year to a cynipid gall wasp in the United Kingdom (Collins et al. 1983, as cited in WPSM _Quercus_).

### Wood-pasture and parkland stem densities

Roellig et al. 2018 (_Rangeland Ecol Manage_ 71: 526-535, DOI 10.1016/j.rama.2018.04.004) _[repository copy, University of Arizona, final published version, full text]_: **393 plots** in eight countries (not 390), at least 55 tree species, plot sizes 0.045-2 ha. Density ranged from **2 trees per ha (central Romania) to almost 5,000 (western Estonia)**, and from 4 to 263 per ha within southern Sweden. Country means (Table 2): Estonia 946 per ha, Germany 877, Hungary 687, Greece 252, Italy 190, Sweden 84, Portugal 76 (cork oak, one species), Romania 8. Five clusters (Table 3, mean density and mean DBH): cluster 1 about **990 per ha at 14.7 cm** (dense, small, right-skewed); cluster 2 about 480 per ha at 21 cm; cluster 3 about **7 per ha at 73 cm** (sparse veterans, almost all central Romania); cluster 4 about 81 per ha at 29 cm; cluster 5 about 90 per ha at 33 cm (both roughly normal DBH, homogeneous). The authors read the DBH distributions as a regeneration signal: sparse stands of large trees (Romania) lack young trees and risk losing their semi-open character; dense small-tree stands (Estonia) have too much regeneration. Oak was among the three main species in nearly every country; birch dominated in Sweden and Estonia, beech in northern Germany. Grazing showed no clear pattern in cluster membership. **Use:** the parkland look has numbers: a veteran end near 8 per ha at 70 cm or more mean DBH, typical grazed wood-pasture at 80-250 per ha and 25-35 cm, and an overgrown end near 1,000 per ha at 15 cm. These are mixed-species, mostly oak, with plot placement that was subjective, so they are a range, not a distribution.

## 4. What this means for the look from the side

The only side-view-relevant measurements I found are the Uholka ones in section 3: 435 trees per hectare (about one per 23 m², or roughly 4.8 m apart on average), mostly small, in a reversed-J diameter distribution, with three layers and gaps no bigger than one crown, over a regeneration carpet of about 3 per m² that is mostly under 40 cm tall. Roellig et al. (section 3) anchors the parkland look: roughly 8 to 1,000 trees per hectare across European wood-pastures, typical grazed ones at 80-250 per hectare with 25-35 cm DBH. The garden's recruits are individually drawn trees from establishment, so it shows none of the sub-metre carpet; I did not convert the garden's `maxTrees` to a per-hectare density (it needs the slab area).

## Implications for the model

These are candidates for the decision in #85, not settled values.

1. **Seed from age and size, not size alone.** Keep `maturity` as a height gate but add (or check against) a minimum age, so a fast-grown recruit cannot seed at 5 years. Candidate minimum ages from section 2, stand-grown rather than open-grown where the source splits them: willow 5–10, birch 5–10 (flora, flowering) to 15 (WPSM; use about 10–15, my judgment), holly 5–12 (use about 10), rowan 15, pine 5–15 (use about 15–20 for stand trees, my judgment), hornbeam 10–30 (use about 25), oak robur 20 (WPSM) to 40 (petraea), fir 25–30, beech 60–80 in stands (40–50 open), sycamore unsourced. The ordering, not the exact years, is what the sources support: willow, holly and birch together at the early end (birch is no longer clearly later than holly now that the flora gives 5–10), then rowan, pine, hornbeam, oak, fir, beech.
2. **Masting as a multiplier on `cSeed` yield.** Per-species crop interval from section 2: sycamore, rowan, birch and hornbeam near 1–3, oak robur 2–4 (petraea 5–7), fir 2–3, pine 4–6, beech 5–10, in synchronised years across the stand. The Czech beech data give the size: a mast year about 10 to 50 times an ordinary one, so a lognormal or two-state year effect would match. For the long-run mean, Watt (1923, via Packham et al.) gives beech and oak 16-17% of a full crop and ash 33%; no source supports "a few percent", so set the ordinary-year level from that mean and the mast spread from the Czech counts. For birch, the average year is about 15% of the normal-maximum crop and under 1% of the largest (Sarvas via Atkinson), which supports a birch year effect with a mean near 0.15 of a good year; it is one experimental series, so treat the frequency of spikes as unsourced. Synchrony within a species matters more than the exact shape.
3. **Add a seedling bank and its loss curve.** (At 2-3% light, beech juveniles survive about 72 years on average and the maples 47-48; Petrovska et al.) A recruit should spend its first seasons as a suppressed state (not an established tree) with a short lifetime when ground light is below its survival light. For beech, the Czech data suggest: high first- and second-year loss (about 40–60% in the second year in cut plots), then 1–15% a year under the canopy. For light-demanders, the survival-light bands in the species-traits note imply near-total loss in shade. A time constant now has an analogue: at about 2% light, potted paper and yellow birch seedlings lost about half in year 1 and every survivor in year 2 (Walters & Reich), so a birch recruit below its survival light should last about 1 to 2 seasons, while about 10% (oak) to 20% (the tolerants) survive two years. These are potted American seedlings in uniform light, so read them as an order of magnitude and a ranking (birch dies first), not a calibrated rate; they are far shorter than the 47-72 year mean survival times Petrovska et al. give for taller shade-tolerant juveniles, because small seedlings and taller juveniles differ. Birch gains from a gap: at 8% light its two-year survival (about 70-80% in year 2) beat the tolerants'. Atkinson adds that birch seedlings lose 80-90% in their first year even outside deep shade, so a first-year establishment probability well below `pEst` = 0.3 is supported for birch if `pEst` is meant to cover the first year; that needs a run before any change.
4. **Cap what shows.** Display or count only recruits taller than a threshold, say 1.3 m, against a bank that is mostly under 40 cm in the real forest (69% under 40 cm at Uholka). A visible-stem target is then 435 per hectare over 6 cm DBH in a primeval beech forest (a closed old wood, the top of the range), 313 per hectare over 5 cm in a browsed oak and birch wood.
5. **Parkland preset anchor.** If a preset aims at a wood-pasture look, use Roellig et al.: 80-250 trees per ha at 25-35 cm DBH (typical), about 8 per ha at 70 cm (veteran), about 1,000 per ha at 15 cm (overgrown). Their caution applies: the sparse end signals missing regeneration.
6. **Rain should not exceed local seeding for shade-tolerants.** At Uholka, willows and poplars are very rare and found only at edges, so a woodland interior should not receive rain of light-demanders; keep rain for willow and birch only for gaps (their `qEst` of 0.3 and 0.25 already restrict this) and consider zero for interior positions. For birch the flora supports this: seed mostly falls within 40-50 m of the parent, the seed bank lasts about a year, and birch seldom regenerates under its own or other canopies.

### Diagnostics to run

- **Age and height at first seed** per species in the current model (baseline), against the table above. This is the first number to get, and it needs a run (not done here).
- **Bank-to-tree ratio**: stems over 1.3 m per hectare equivalent over time in the garden's closed state; the real stands show 435 per hectare over 6 cm DBH.
- **Share of recruits that survive their first 5, 20 seasons** per species under a closed canopy and in a gap; compare the Czech beech curve for beech, and test that birch, willow and pine recruits mostly die in shade (for birch, within about 1 to 2 seasons at about 2% light, from Walters & Reich).
- **Mast-year spread**: ratio of the best to median year's recruit count per species, expecting about 10 or more for beech and oak.
- **Species mix of recruits** in a closed stand: shade-tolerants (beech, hornbeam, maple, fir) should dominate the bank, light-demanders appear only in gaps.
